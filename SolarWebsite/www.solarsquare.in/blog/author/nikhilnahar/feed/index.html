What is a 110W Solar Panel and How Does it Work?

A 110W solar panel is a photovoltaic module rated to produce 110W under Standard Test Conditions (STC) in a lab. In real use, it generates DC power that varies with sunlight intensity and temperature.

Most 110W panels are labeled 12V nominal. This does not mean the panel produces exactly 12 volts. It means the panel is designed to charge a 12V battery using a solar charge controller. The actual operating voltage is usually higher than 12V.

Always remember these key parameters about a 110W solar panel before making a purchase:

  • 110W refers to the peak-rated output under lab conditions.
  • 12V nominal means the operating voltage is above 12V during battery charging.
  • It is commonly used for small off-grid systems and backup setups.
  • You need a charge controller and a battery to store power for night use.

How Does a 110W Solar Panel Work?

A 110-watt solar panel, regardless of its cell type, uses the photovoltaic effect to convert solar energy into DC power, which can be stored in a battery for later use.

Here’s a step-by-step guide on how the panel works:

  • Step 1 – Sunlight hits the solar cells: Sunlight contains tiny energy particles called photons. When these photons strike the silicon solar cells inside the panel, the cells absorb that energy.
  • Step 2 – Electrons get energized: The absorbed energy knocks electrons within the solar cells loose from their atoms. This movement creates free electrons and leaves behind positive holes. Each energized electron creates an electron-hole pair.
  • Step 3 – The internal electric field creates voltage: Inside the solar cell, a built-in electric field at the p-n junction forces electrons and holes to move in opposite directions. This separation creates voltage across the cell.
  • Step 4 – Electrons flow as direct current (DC): When you connect the panel to a circuit, the free electrons move in one direction through the external wires. This one-way flow of electrons is direct current.
  • Step 5 – The charge controller regulates the output: Before charging a battery, the DC power passes through a charge controller. It stabilizes voltage and current, preventing overcharging and protecting connected devices.

Is a 110-watt Solar Panel Suitable to Build a Rooftop Solar System for Homes?

A 110W solar panel is not practical for building a kilowatt-scale rooftop solar system for homes. Even for a small 3 kW solar system, you would need ~28. Most residential rooftops in India do not have that kind of shadow-free space to accommodate so many panels.

Today, higher-wattage panels like Mono PERC and TOPCon are widely available. These panels offer better efficiency, require fewer modules to reach the same capacity, and reduce overall installation complexity. And because you need fewer panels, mounting structures, wiring, and labor costs are also lower. Therefore, these panels are most recommended for residential and commercial rooftop solar systems.

Here are the main reasons why a 110W panel is not recommended for a kW-scale rooftop system:

  • Too many panels required: A higher system capacity requires combining multiple panels. Using 110W modules requires installing a large number of panels, increasing space requirements and system complexity.
  • Limited rooftop space: Most homes have restricted usable roof area due to water tanks, shading, and structural layout. Small panels make it harder to optimize that space efficiently.
  • Lower efficiency compared to modern panels: Most 110W panels use older cell technologies. Modern high-wattage panels such as TOPCon deliver up to 26% efficiency, producing more power from the same roof area.
  • Higher installation costs: More panels mean more mounting structures, wiring connections, and labor. This increases the total installation cost compared to using fewer high-wattage modules.

FAQs

How many amps does a 110W solar panel produce?

A 110W solar panel can produce ~6 amps under ideal sunlight. Using the formula Amps = Watts ÷ Volts, at an operating voltage of 18V, it generates about 6.1 amps. When charging a 12V battery at 14V, it may reach 7 to 8 amps. Actual output varies with sunlight and temperature conditions.

Why does the 110W solar panel price vary between brands?

The price varies because brands differ in build quality, cell technology, efficiency, warranty coverage, and after-sales support. Some panels use better glass, stronger frames, and higher-grade solar cells, which improve durability and performance but also raise costs.

Is a 110W solar panel enough to run a home?

No, a 110W solar panel is not enough to run a full home. It is suitable only for small loads, such as LED lights, a Wi-Fi router, phone charging, or a small DC fan. Running major appliances such as refrigerators, air conditioners, or water pumps requires a much larger solar system.

Can a 110W solar panel charge a 12V battery?

Yes, a 110W solar panel can charge a 12V battery when connected through a proper charge controller. Most 110W panels are designed as 12V nominal panels. Hence, their operating voltage is suitable for safely charging 12V battery systems.

Can I connect two 110-watt panels to one 12V battery?

Yes, you can connect two 110W panels to one 12V battery, but you must use a suitable charge controller for that.

  • For most 12V systems, panels are connected in parallel. This keeps the voltage constant while increasing current.
  • Connecting panels in series increases voltage. This can work with an MPPT charge controller, but only if the controller’s maximum PV input voltage is not exceeded.

Always check the charge controller’s voltage and current limits before connecting multiple panels.

What is an On-Grid, an Off-Grid, and a Hybrid Solar System?

All three are PV solar systems that use the photovoltaic effect to convert solar radiation into solar electricity. While the process of converting sunlight into electricity remains the same across all three, it’s their connectivity to the grid and batteries that result in primary differences

Let’s explore all three of them closely.

What is an On-Grid Solar System?

An on-grid solar system is a grid-tied PV system that is connected to the grid and does not use solar rechargeable batteries to store energy for later use

  • During the day, any extra power generated by the panels that is not used in real time is sent to the grid through a bi-directional net meter or smart meter
  • When solar generation is low or at night, the home draws electricity from the grid.

The net meter records this two-way energy exchange between the grid and the on-grid solar system, and the process is known as net metering

What is an Off-Grid Solar System?

An off-grid solar system is designed for areas where grid power is unavailable or highly unreliable. It operates completely independently of the grid and relies on solar panels and battery storage to offer an uninterrupted power supply to your home.

Since this system is the only source of electricity, it must be designed for full autonomy.

Here’s what that means:

  • More solar panels are installed: The system is intentionally oversized so it can generate enough energy even during low sunlight conditions.
  • Battery backup is critical: The batteries must store enough energy to power the home for at least 2 days without solar generation.
  • A higher-capacity inverter is required: Since more power is generated and stored, the inverter must be able to handle higher loads safely.

And that’s why an off-grid solar system is the most expensive of all three types.

What is a Hybrid Solar System?

A hybrid rooftop solar energy system for homes is connected to the grid and a battery bank. The electricity generated by the solar panels is used in these ways:

  • Required power is used in the house in real time: This is the solar electricity that keeps your household appliances running during the daytime.
  • Additional power is used to charge the battery bank: Power not used during the day is used to charge the solar battery so it can be used at night or during periods of low solar generation
  • Handling leftover power once the batteries are full: If there’s still any power left after the batteries are charged, it can be sent directly to the grid.

Battery sizing in a hybrid solar system is critical and must be done based on the duration of power cuts in your area.

Let’s give you an example-

  • A 2.5 kWh battery usually provides about 1 hour of backup for a 3 kW system.
  • If your area experiences daily 2-3-hour outages, this capacity will not be sufficient.
  • In such cases, a 5 kWh battery is more suitable to ensure an uninterrupted supply.

On-Grid vs Off-Grid vs Hybrid Solar Systems: How They Work?

All three solar systems use the photovoltaic effect to generate electricity. But the way that electricity is used and stored differs

Let’s have a look at how all three systems work step by step

How Does an On-Grid Solar System Work?

Solar panels absorb sunlight and convert it into DC power, which is then sent to a solar inverter for conversion to AC power for home use.

Here’s a step-by-step summary:

  • Solar cells in PV panels absorb sunlight: When sunlight hits the panels, photons transfer their energy to electrons in the solar cells, exciting them.
  • Excited electrons begin moving: The p-n junction in the solar cell ensures that electrons move in a single direction, creating a flow of current.
  • DC electricity is generated: This movement of electrons produces direct current (DC) electricity.
  • DC is converted to AC: The solar inverter converts DC power into alternating current (AC), which is suitable for household appliances.
  • Excess electricity is sent to the grid: Any power not used in real time is exported to the grid through a net meter.
  • Net metering tracks usage and export: If your system exports more electricity than it imports, you receive credits from the DISCOM. If you import more than you export, you pay for the additional units consumed.

How Does a Hybrid Solar System Work?

A hybrid solar system works similarly to an on-grid system in the initial stages, where solar panels generate electricity and convert sunlight into DC power. However, the key difference lies in how excess energy is stored and used.

Here’s a step-by-step breakdown:

  • Solar panels generate DC electricity: Sunlight is absorbed by the solar panels, producing direct current (DC) electricity.
  • DC power is converted to AC: The hybrid solar inverter converts DC power to alternating current (AC), which powers your home in real time.
  • Excess energy is stored in batteries: Any unused power is directed to the battery through the inverter (which also acts as a solar charge controller), storing energy for later use.
  • Additional surplus is exported to the grid: If the batteries are fully charged, the remaining electricity is sent to the grid via net metering.
  • Power supply during outages: When a power cut occurs, the system automatically switches to battery mode and supplies stored solar energy to your home.
  • Power supply at night or during low generation: During nighttime or when solar generation is insufficient, the system first draws on stored battery energy, then draws from the grid if needed.

How Does an Off-Grid Solar System Work?

An off-grid solar system operates completely independently of the electricity grid. It relies entirely on solar panels and battery storage to generate and supply power to your home.

Here’s a step-by-step breakdown:

  • Solar panels generate DC electricity: Sunlight is absorbed by the solar panels, producing direct current (DC) electricity.
  • DC power is converted to AC: The solar inverter converts DC electricity into alternating current (AC), which is used to run household appliances in real time.
  • Excess energy is stored in batteries: Any unused electricity is stored in a solar battery bank for later use, ensuring power availability even when sunlight is not available.
  • Power supply during the night or low generation: When solar panels are not generating enough electricity, such as at night or during cloudy weather, the system draws power from the stored battery energy.
  • The system is designed for full autonomy: Since there is no grid backup, it is intentionally sized with additional panels and larger batteries to ensure the home can run for at least 2 days without solar generation.

Can You Convert Off-Grid to On-Grid Solar Systems?

Yes, an off-grid solar system can be converted to an on-grid or grid-connected system in many cases, but there are a few technical considerations you need to understand.

In the Indian market, most off-grid systems are not built using off-grid inverters. Instead, installers use a hybrid inverter with a higher capacity so it can:

  • Handle a larger number of solar panels.
  • Manage higher power flow without overloading.
  • Operate independently when the grid supply is unavailable.

Because hybrid solar inverters already support grid connectivity, these off-grid solar PV systems can later be connected to the grid and converted to on-grid.

While this conversion is technically possible, the system may not remain optimally sized after it is converted to on-grid. Here’s why:

  • Off-grid systems are designed for full autonomy.
  • This requires more solar panels to generate excess energy.
  • It also requires larger battery storage to provide backup for at least 2 days.
  • A higher-capacity inverter is used to handle this load.

Once grid supply becomes available, this setup often becomes oversized for actual needs.

To better understand this, let’s look at a practical example.

Consider a homestay in a remote village of Uttarakhand with a load requirement of 5 kW. Since grid supply is either unavailable or extremely unreliable, the system must be designed for full autonomy. This means:

  • Installing more solar panels
  • Adding a larger battery bank
  • Using a higher-capacity inverter to ensure uninterrupted power even when there is no sunlight.

Now compare this with a similar 5 kW load in a metro city like Delhi or Mumbai, where grid supply is available but power cuts last for only 2-3 hours. In this case, the system does not need to be oversized. A standard 5 kW inverter (with or without a moderate battery backup) is sufficient because the grid is available as a fallback.

If grid supply becomes available later at the Uttarakhand location, the existing system can technically be connected to the grid, as the hybrid inverter supports it. However, the system will remain oversized because it was originally designed to operate without any grid support. This means higher-than-necessary panel capacity and battery backup, which may not be cost-efficient for a grid-connected setup.

All in all, while converting an off-grid system to an on-grid system is possible in India, the system design may not remain optimal after conversion. This is why it is important to plan your solar system based on long-term grid availability rather than current conditions alone.

What is a Solar Junction Box and Where is it Located?

A solar junction box is a sealed, weatherproof box mounted on the back of a solar panel. It serves as the connection point where the panel’s internal wiring (from the solar cells) connects to the external cables that carry electricity to the rest of the solar system.

Simply put, a solar panel junction box is the connection between the silicon cells of the panels generating DC power and the DC solar cables that carry that power to your inverter, charge controller, or distribution box.

You’ll find the solar panel junction box attached to the backsheet (the TPT layer) of the solar panel with the help of a silicone adhesive. It’s a small rectangular box, roughly the size of a bar of soap. Two cables come out of it, one positive and one negative. Each cable has a plug at the end called an MC4 connector, which is used to connect the panel to other panels or to the inverter.

The solar junction box also contains:

  • Thin copper strips that carry electricity from the solar cells.
  • Bypass diodes that help electricity flow properly if part of the panel is shaded.
  • A sealant or rubber lining that keeps moisture and dust out.

So, when someone asks what is a junction box in a solar panel, the short answer is it’s the electrical output interface of the module, located on the back of the panel.

  • Split (three-part) junction box: These are three smaller boxes instead of one, each handling part of the panel. It improves heat dissipation and is widely used in modern half-cut panels.

Solar J Boxes Based on Potting

How a solar panel junction box is sealed on the inside plays a major role in its lifespan. This sealing method, called potting, affects moisture protection, heat handling, and overall durability.

Here are the two types based on whether they’re potted or not:

  • Non-potted junction boxes: They rely on a rubber O-ring or silicone gasket to seal the lid. They’re cheaper and easier to service, but they depend on the gasket staying elastic over decades.
  • Potted junction boxes: They are filled with silicone potting compound that completely encapsulates the diodes and terminals. The potting blocks moisture, reduces vibration damage, improves heat transfer away from the diodes, and seals the box for life. Almost all types of outdoor solar modules today use potted solar junction boxes because they are designed for harsh weather conditions.

Solar Panel J Boxes Based on IP Rating

The IP (Ingress Protection) rating of a solar junction box indicates how well it resists dust and water over years of outdoor exposure. Since the solar PV junction box is the most exposed electrical point on the panel, this rating directly impacts safety, performance, and lifespan.

The two digits in the IP rating indicate protection against solid particles and liquids, which is critical in conditions such as heavy rain, dust, and humidity.

Here are the main types of photovoltaic junction boxes based on IP ratings:

  • IP65 junction box: It’s dust-tight and protected against water jets, and is considered the minimum standard for rooftop solar installations.
  • IP67 junction box: It’s dust-tight and protected against temporary water immersion, and is suitable for areas with heavy rainfall.
  • IP68 junction box: It’s dust-tight, designed for continuous water immersion, and used in extreme environments like floating solar systems or coastal regions.

What are the Different Components in a Junction Box for Solar PV Modules?

Open a solar panel junction box, and you’ll find different parts, each playing a critical role in carrying current, protecting the panel, and keeping everything sealed for years.

Every component inside is designed to handle heat, moisture, and continuous electrical flow.

  • Enclosure (box and lid): This is the outer casing of the solar junction box, made of heat and fire-resistant plastic. Its main job is to protect all internal parts from weather and physical damage.
  • Sealing system: This is the rubber gasket or a sealing layer between the box and lid. Its main job is to keep water, dust, and humidity out of the junction box.
  • Breather membrane in some designs: It allows heat and pressure to escape while blocking moisture from getting inside.
  • Terminal rails or blocks: These are copper connection points where the panel’s internal wires are attached and routed outward.
  • Bypass diodes: These are small electronic components that protect the panel from overheating when part of it is shaded.
  • Internal wiring, aka cell ribbons: These are thin, conductive strips that carry current from the solar cells to the solar panel junction box.
  • Cable entry seal: This is the sealed point where cables leave the box, ensuring no moisture enters.
  • Potting or internal sealing compound: It’s made of silicone or resin material that encapsulates components, improves durability, and protects against vibration and moisture.

What are the Main Functions of a PV Junction Box?

Despite its small size, a solar junction box plays multiple roles. It handles electrical transfer, protection, sealing, and mechanical stability, all in one compact unit. Without it, a solar panel cannot safely deliver power to the rest of the solar energy system.

Here’s a snapshot of all critical functions carried out by a solar DC junction box:

  • Takes DC power out of the panel: It collects DC electricity from the solar cells and sends it to the external cables.
  • Prevents panel damage: Bypass diodes inside it stop overheating when part of the panel is shaded.
  • Keeps water and dust out: The sealed box protects all connections from rain, heat, and dirt.
  • Holds cables securely: It keeps cables in place so they do not loosen or break due to wind or movement.
  • Helps manage heat: It reduces heat buildup around the diodes, helping the panel work reliably over time.

How to Connect a Solar Junction Box to Solar Panels?

The most important thing you need to understand about installing a solar junction box is that it’s not a DIY (do it yourself) task. Although not illegal, you should not attempt to install a solar junction box yourself, as it involves live DC wiring, polarity-sensitive components, and sealing requirements that directly affect safety and panel lifespan.

Installers do not open or modify the solar junction box during installation, as it comes pre-assembled, sealed, and tested by the manufacturer. Any work inside the solar J box, such as replacing or reconnecting internal components, is done during manufacturing or by trained technicians during maintenance.

Here’s how professionals handle solar panel junction box connections at the panel level when assembly or maintenance is required:

  • They isolate the panel before working: They reduce exposure to light and follow safety procedures, but still treat all wiring as live. Before installing the solar junction box, installers always make sure the solar system is not generating power.
  • They identify and verify polarity: They check which ribbon is positive and which is negative. Then, the positive ribbon is connected to the positive terminal and the negative ribbon to the negative terminal inside the PV junction box.
  • They connect cell ribbons to terminals: They solder or clamp the internal conductors onto terminal rails inside the junction box.
  • They install and orient bypass diodes correctly: They place diodes across the terminals with proper polarity to prevent reverse current and overheating.
  • They secure cable exits and seals: They ensure the cable entry points are tight and sealed to maintain moisture protection.
  • They seal the junction box completely: They use potting compound so the box remains waterproof and dustproof for years.
  • They bond the box to the panel backsheet: They secure the solar junction box with industrial adhesive to ensure long-term mechanical stability.

Mistakes to Avoid When Installing a Junction Box for a PV Module

Most solar junction box failures occur due to minor mistakes during manufacturing or handling. The errors listed below can lead to water ingress, electrical faults, and even panel damage.

  • Skipping polarity checks: Connecting positive and negative incorrectly can damage bypass diodes and, in some cases, the solar inverter.
  • Over-tightening cable seals: Excess force can crack the plastic or damage the seal, allowing moisture to enter over time.
  • Under-tightening cable seals: Loose fittings let cables move or pull during intense cyclonic winds.
  • Using non-UV-rated cables: Standard cables degrade quickly in sunlight, leading to insulation failure within a few years.
  • Poor junction box placement: Installing the box where water can collect increases the risk of leaks and seal failure.
  • Incomplete sealing at the base: Not applying the proper adhesive or sealant between the box and backsheet can leave gaps for moisture to enter.
  • Opening a sealed junction box in the field: Breaking the factory seal can void the warranty of the solar panel.

This answer depends on the voltage. Amperes (amps) measure electrical current, and the relationship between kilowatts and amps depends on the voltage of your circuit.

The full formula becomes, Amperes = (Kilowatts x 1000) ÷ (Voltage x Power Factor)

Most household appliances have a power factor between 0.8 and 1.

This is the geyser scenario. A 2 kW geyser running for 30 minutes in the morning uses 1 unit. If your family has four people each taking a hot shower with 30 minutes of geyser heating, you are looking at 4 units a day just for hot water, or 120 units a month. In peak winter, that can climb to 150+ units, which is why geysers are usually the second-biggest line item on a North Indian electricity bill.

Why is it Important to Understand the Difference Between kW and kWh to Read Electricity Bills?

Once you understand the difference between kW and kWh, three things become much clearer.

  • You stop blaming the wrong appliances: A 1.5 kW AC running for 6 hours uses far more electricity than a 2 kW geyser running for 20 minutes. Power rating alone does not predict cost. Time of use matters just as much.
  • You start looking at appliances differently when you shop: A 5-star BEE-rated AC might cost more upfront, but if it consumes 1.2 kW instead of 1.6 kW for the same cooling, you save units every single hour it runs. Over a year, that can add up to thousands of rupees.
  • You can spot phantom load and standby drain: Devices on standby (TVs, set-top boxes, microwave displays, or chargers left plugged in) draw small amounts of power 24 hours a day. Individually, they are tiny, but a household with 10 devices each drawing 5 watts on standby wastes 1.2 units every day. That is 36 units a month.

Why Are Electricity Bills in kWh, Not kW?

Kilowatt (kW) alone tells the utility company (DISCOM) nothing about how much electricity you actually consumed. A 5 kW connection sitting unused for an entire month consumes zero electricity. The same connection running for 24 hours every day consumes 3,600 kWh.

The DISCOM has no idea which one you did unless they measure the energy over time, which is exactly what kWh does.

This is also why your meter is called an energy meter and not a power meter. It is integrating power over time to give you total consumption. Older mechanical meters did this with a spinning aluminium disc. Modern digital meters do it electronically and can also send the data to your distribution company in real time.

When installing an on-grid rooftop solar system at home, both kW and kWh will show up on every quote, every proposal, and every sales conversation. And they answer two completely different questions about your system.

  • kW tells you the size of your solar system: When installers say they recommend a 5 kW solar system based on your home’s energy consumption, they mean the solar panels can produce up to 5 kilowatts of power at their peak. Since 1 kilowatt is 1,000 watts (how many watts in a kilowatt is the same answer here as anywhere else), a 5 kW PV system is 5,000 watts of combined panel capacity sitting on your roof.
  • kWh tells you how much electricity that system actually generates: This is the number that actually lowers your electricity bill. A 5 kW solar system in India generates 7,000 to 7,250 units of solar electricity in a year. Since this electricity comes from solar energy, which is free, you no longer have to pay hefty bills, as your home is now solar-powered.

How much is 20 kWh of electricity?

Since 1 kWh equals 1 unit, 20 kWh equals 20 units. There is no conversion involved. Whatever your meter shows in kWh is what your bill counts as units.

1 kilowatt of power equals 1,000 watts, which is enough to run 1,000 watts’ worth of appliances simultaneously. And if you use 1 kW of power continuously for 1 hour, you consume exactly 1 unit (1 kWh) of electricity.

How many kW does 1 unit make?

Units and kilowatts measure two different things. 1 unit of electricity is equal to 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh), not 1 kilowatt (kW). The difference is time. A kilowatt is the rate at which an appliance draws power right now, while a unit (kWh) is the total electricity consumed when 1 kilowatt of power runs for 1 full hour.

Is kWh the same as a unit of electricity in India?

Yes, 1 unit on your electricity bill is exactly equal to 1 kWh of energy consumption. This is the standard used by every state distribution company.

How do I calculate kWh or units?

You can calculate kWh or units (since 1 kWh = 1 unit) using a simple formula:

Units = kW x Hours.

First, convert the appliance’s wattage to kilowatts by dividing by 1,000 (e.g., 1,500 W = 1.5 kW). Then, multiply it by the number of hours you use it. For instance, if you run it for 6 hours, that’s 1.5 x 6 = 9 units per day. To estimate monthly usage, multiply by 30 (9 x 30 = 270 units), and to find the cost, multiply total units by the electricity rates in your city.
