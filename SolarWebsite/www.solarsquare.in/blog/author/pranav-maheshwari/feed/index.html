]]> Your rooftop solar system shows you numbers all day: kW, V, A, a blinking light, and sometimes a code that looks like a number plate. Most owners glance at the screen and move on. That is a missed chance, because knowing how to read solar inverter data is the quickest way to tell whether your system is earning its keep or quietly losing units.

To read solar inverter data, start with generation (kW now, kWh today). Next, check that DC (Direct Current) and AC (Alternating Current) voltage and current sit within the ranges in your manual. Then scan for error codes and compare today’s output with a similar sunny day. A pattern that repeats is worth a call to your installer.

This guide walks you through the solar inverter information on your display and app, unpacks the solar inverter technical details behind each reading, and shows which solar inverter details are routine and which deserve a call to your installer.

Where to Find Solar Inverter Information

Before you decode any number, you need to know where the solar inverter information actually lives. Solar inverter information reaches you through three routes, and each one shows a slightly different slice of the picture. Following are the three places where you can find your solar inverter information:

1. Solar Inverter Details on the Inverter’s Own Display

Most string inverters have a small LCD (Liquid Crystal Display) or LED (Light-Emitting Diode) screen. Press the buttons, and you will usually cycle through live power, today’s energy, lifetime energy, voltage, current, and any active fault. It is the simplest source of solar inverter details, and it keeps working even when your Wi-Fi does not.

Solar Inverter Information in the Monitoring App or Web Portal

A Wi-Fi dongle or 4G data logger sends your readings to a phone app or a solar energy monitoring system. This is where solar inverter information becomes useful over time: day-by-day graphs, monthly totals, and fault alerts. If you want to compare today’s solar inverter details with last Tuesday’s, the app is the place to do it.

Your PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana Records

If your plant was installed under the Pradhan Mantri Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana (PM Surya Ghar), a lot of solar inverter information now travels beyond your own app. The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) has issued directions on inverter communication devices, Machine-to-Machine (M2M) SIM-based monitoring, and daily generation data sharing. Your application and commissioning status are on the official PM Surya Ghar portal, and MNRE posts its latest notices at mnre.gov.in.

One thing to remember: your net meter, read by your distribution company (DISCOM), records grid import and export for billing. The inverter records what the panels produced. The two will not match exactly, and that is normal.

Solar Inverter Technical Details Explained: What Each Reading Means

This is where most owners get lost. The solar inverter technical details on your screen fall into three groups: generation, DC input from the panels, and AC output to your home and the grid. Take these solar inverter technical details one at a time.

1. Solar Inverter Details on Generation: kW vs kWh

Kilowatt (kW) is power right now. Kilowatt-hour (kWh) is energy over time, and it is the “unit” on your electricity bill. A plant producing 3 kW for two hours has generated 6 kWh. These solar inverter details are the first thing to check.

Current power (kW) Output at this moment Midday health check
Today’s energy (kWh) Units made since sunrise Comparing similar days
Total energy (kWh) Lifetime generation Long-term trend and payback

2. Solar Inverter Technical Details on the DC Side: Voltage and Current

Panels produce direct current (DC). The photovoltaic (PV) voltage in volts (V) and current in amperes (A) show what your panels are feeding the inverter. Voltage depends mostly on how many panels are connected in a string, while current rises and falls with sunlight. So in the morning, voltage jumps quickly and current climbs slowly.

Most inverters use Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT), which keeps adjusting current and voltage to maximise power output. If your inverter has two inputs (PV1 and PV2), similar readings are generally expected when the connected strings have similar panel counts, orientations, and shading conditions. A big gap is worth checking.

Temperature affects solar-panel performance. The power output of a PV solar module typically decreases as its cell temperature rises, but the rate depends on the module’s temperature coefficient. RETC’s 2025 PV Module Index Report found that more than 52% of the tested module models met its high-achiever criterion for temperature coefficient, defined as better than −0.3% per °C. This means the power loss varies by panel technology and design, so check the temperature coefficient on your module’s datasheet.

Source: Renewable Energy Test Center (RETC), 2025 PV Module Index Report

3. Solar Inverter Information on the AC Side: Voltage, Current and Frequency

Alternating current (AC) readings show how the inverter is feeding your home and the grid. Voltage should stay close to the grid’s nominal value, and frequency (in hertz, Hz) should hover near 50 Hz. If the grid drifts out of range, the inverter pauses export on purpose. That is a safety feature, and the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) covers connectivity and protection expectations in its technical standards for distributed generation.

The exact limits for your model are in its datasheet, so keep the solar inverter technical details handy: maximum DC voltage, MPPT range, and rated AC output.

How to Read Solar Inverter Data for Performance

Numbers mean little on their own, and solar inverter information only helps when you know how to read and compare it. Performance is about comparison, so this section shows how to read solar inverter data against the weather, the season and your own history.

Read the Daily Curve

On a clear day, power should rise through the morning, peak near noon, and fall gently by evening, like a smooth bell. Passing clouds cause short dips that recover quickly, and that is normal.

Pattern What it usually means
Smooth bell curve Healthy day
Brief dips, quick recovery Passing clouds
Flat top well below normal Clipping, shading or a limit on output
Drops to zero in daylight Grid outage, trip or fault

Compare Solar Inverters on the Same Parameters

One day tells you little. Compare clear days with clear days, and monsoon weeks with monsoon weeks. A slow slide in output over several clear weeks often points to dust on the panels, while a sudden step down points to a fault or new shading. These solar inverter details help you separate weather from hardware, and they show how to read solar inverter data like a trend, not a snapshot.

Performance Ratio: Solar Inverter Technical Details in Plain Words

Solar performance ratio (PR) compares the energy your plant actually delivered with the energy it should have delivered for the sunlight it received. It is defined in an international standard and used by most solar companies to judge plant health. Homes rarely have a sunlight sensor, so ask your installer for your plant’s expected monthly generation and track against that.

Solar Inverter Technical Details: Peak Efficiency Is Not Annual Yield

A datasheet may quote a very high maximum efficiency. That is a best-case point, not what you get all year. Among the solar inverter technical details, treat it as a comparison tool between models, not a promise about your bill.

  • Note the time and photograph the code before it clears.
  • Restart only as your manual describes.
  • Never open the inverter or handle DC cables yourself.
  • Call your installer if you see zero daylight generation, repeated faults, or a steady output drop with no weather reason.
  • Keep your inverter’s model and serial number ready.

Your 5-Minute Weekly Check: Solar Inverter Information and Details

Check the following 5 things every week to make sure that your home rooftop solar is working properly:

  1. Note today’s kWh and compare it with a similar sunny day.
  2. Look at the midday kW peak.
  3. Check DC voltage and current on every input.
  4. Check AC voltage and frequency.
  5. Scan the fault log.

Make Your Solar Inverter Data Work for You

Reading your inverter comes down to three habits: check generation (kW and kWh), compare voltage and current against your manual, and never ignore a repeated error. These quick checks take about five minutes and can help you spot dust, shading, or faults before they reduce solar generation.

That is the heart of how to read solar inverter data: compare, don’t just look. A smooth daily curve and steady monthly totals mean your plant is doing its job. A change with no weather reason means it is time to ask questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are normal solar inverter technical details for voltage and current?

They vary by model and string size. DC voltage should sit within the MPPT range printed in your datasheet, and AC voltage should stay near the grid’s nominal value. Current follows sunlight.

Why does my solar inverter show zero generation?

At night, it is expected for your solar inverter to show zero generation. In daylight, the usual causes are a grid outage (the inverter shuts down for safety), a tripped breaker, or a fault. If it persists, contact your solar installer.

What is the difference between kW and kWh on a solar inverter?

kW (kilowatts) measures how much power your solar system produces at a given moment, while kWh (kilowatt-hours) measures how much electricity it generates over time. For example, a 3 kW solar system running at full capacity for one hour would generate 3 kWh (3 units) of electricity. Your savings depend on kWh. Read in detail about kW vs. kWh here.

How do I know from solar inverter data if my system is working properly?

Check your solar inverter’s daily energy generation (kWh) to see how much electricity your system produces. Compare it with its usual output under similar weather conditions. Also, check for error messages, warning lights, or unexpected drops in generation. If the output stays unusually low despite good sunlight, contact your solar installer for a system check.

Why is my solar inverter output lower than its rated capacity?

Your solar inverter may produce less than its rated capacity due to cloudy weather, high temperatures, dust on panels, shading, or normal system losses. The rated capacity is the maximum power it can handle under specified conditions, not a constant output. Check your system’s generation under similar weather conditions before assuming there is a fault.

What should I do when my inverter shows an error code?

Note the code, check your manual, and try only the safe steps listed there. Call your installer for ground faults, over-voltage, or repeated errors.

Can I check solar inverter information on my phone?

Yes, if your inverter has a Wi-Fi or SIM-based communication device. Under PM Surya Ghar, MNRE has issued rules on such devices, so confirm yours is set up.

]]> Did you know that scientists can now print solar cells the same way a newspaper is printed? Australia’s national science agency, CSIRO (Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation), has even built a dedicated printed photovoltaic facility to do exactly this, rolling out solar cells onto thin plastic sheets.

Printable solar panels are thin, bendable solar cells made by printing a special solar ink onto plastic or film, rather than cutting them from silicon wafers. They can be produced on rolls, much like newspapers or banknotes, making them light, bendable, and cheaper to manufacture at scale.

In this blog, we will break down what printable solar panels actually are, how they are made, where they are used, their pros and cons, and where this technology is headed next. Whether you are a curious homeowner or someone tracking the future of solar energy in India, this guide will give you a clear, honest picture.

What Are Printable Solar Panels?

Printable solar panels are solar cells made by printing light-absorbing materials, like organic compounds or perovskites, onto thin, flexible surfaces like plastic film. Instead of slicing silicon into rigid wafers, manufacturers dissolve special solar materials into an ink and print them directly onto a surface.

Printed solar panels are very different from how traditional solar panels are made. A standard silicon solar panel starts with a silicon ingot, sliced into thin wafers, processed, and assembled into a rigid glass-covered panel. It is a heavy, multi-step industrial process.

Printable solar cells replace silicon wafers with a printable “solar ink.” This ink is made from light-sensitive compounds and can be applied by standard printing techniques. As a result, printable solar panels are lightweight sheets that can bend, curve, and be rolled up.

Because the core function remains the same as any solar cell, capturing sunlight and converting it into electricity, this technology is sometimes called printable photovoltaic cells. It’s just the manufacturing process that changes.

How Do Printable Solar Panels Work?

Printable solar panels work by using solar inks made from light-absorbing materials. These materials are deposited layer-by-layer onto flexible film using techniques like inkjet, slot-die, or screen printing. Once printed, these layers form a working solar cell that generates electricity when exposed to sunlight.

Printable solar panels are made by using materials like:

  • Organic photovoltaics (OPV): Organic photovoltaic cells are carbon-based polymer-fullerene compounds that can be dissolved into inks and printed onto flexible sheets.
  • Perovskites: Perovskites are crystal-structured materials that have seen the fastest efficiency growth of any solar technology in history. These are synthetic or mixed organic-inorganic structures that can be dissolved into liquid inks. According to the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), perovskite solar cells improved from around 3% efficiency in 2009 to over 25% within roughly a decade.

Source: NREL

The Printing Process of Print Solar Panels

The printing process of print solar panels includes ink preparation, plastic films, printing through industrial printing techniques, layering, sealing, and then testing.

  1. Ink preparation: The active solar material (organic compound or perovskite) is dissolved into a liquid ink.
  2. Substrate loading: A long roll of thin plastic film is fed into the printing system.
  3. Roll-to-roll printing: The ink is applied onto the moving film using industrial printing techniques, similar to how newspapers are printed at scale.
  4. Layering: Additional functional layers (electrodes, protective coatings) are printed or deposited on top.
  5. Curing and sealing: The printed sheet is dried and sealed to protect it from moisture and air.
  6. Testing: Each printed section is tested for performance before being cut and packaged.

CSIRO researchers built a system capable of manufacturing and testing more than 10,000 printed solar cells a day, which helped them quickly identify the best settings for large-scale roll-to-roll production. This is the scale and speed that makes printable solar panels commercially exciting.

Types of Printable Solar Technology

OPV cells use carbon-based, plastic-like compounds. They are extremely lightweight, low-cost to produce, and can even be made semi-transparent, useful for windows and building facades. Their efficiency, however, remains lower than other options.

Perovskites are currently the fastest-improving printable solar technology. CSIRO’s roll-to-roll printed perovskite modules achieved 15.5% efficiency for small cells and 11% for a 50 cm² printed module, a record for fully printed solar cells at that scale. In lab conditions, NREL has certified individual perovskite cells above 25% efficiency, though these are not yet commercial products.

Advantages of Printable Solar Panels

Printable solar panels offer several real advantages over traditional rigid panels, especially around cost, weight, and manufacturing speed.

  • Low manufacturing cost: Printing eliminates the need for silicon wafer processing, which lowers material and production costs. Researchers estimate that once roll-to-roll perovskite production is optimised, costs could fall per watt, still more than silicon, but a significant step toward affordability at scale.
  • Lightweight and flexible: Because they are printed on thin plastic film rather than glass, these panels can bend around curved surfaces.
  • Faster, scalable production: Roll-to-roll printing works much like printing newspapers, allowing continuous, high-speed manufacturing rather than the slower, batch-based process used for silicon panels.
  • New use cases: Their flexibility opens doors that rigid silicon panels simply cannot, curved rooftops, wearable devices, and semi-transparent windows, to name a few.

Limitations and Challenges of Printable Solar Panels

The biggest challenge with printable solar panels right now is durability, not performance. While printable solar panels’ efficiency has improved rapidly in the lab, most printed cells still degrade faster than silicon when exposed to real-world heat, humidity, and sunlight over years.

  • Lower efficiency compared to silicon: Most commercial printed solar cells still fall short of the efficiency that mainstream silicon panels offer today.
  • Durability and lifespan concerns: Silicon panels routinely last ~25 years or more. Printed perovskite and organic cells, by contrast, have historically lasted only months. Though the lifespan of printed solar panels can be improved with better sealing and materials.
  • Moisture and UV degradation: Because these cells are thinner and often lack the protective glass layer of traditional panels, they are more vulnerable to moisture ingress and UV damage over time.
  • Commercial scalability still developing: Scaling from lab success to a stable, mass-market solar panel printout remains a work in progress.

Real-World Applications of Printable Solar Panels

Even at this early stage, printable solar cells are already being tested and used in situations where traditional panels simply cannot go.

  • Building-integrated solar: Semi-transparent organic photovoltaic cells can be integrated into windows and facades, generating power without blocking light entirely.
  • Wearable electronics: Thin, flexible printable photovoltaic cells can be woven into clothing or accessories to power small electronics on the move.
  • Portable and off-grid chargers: Lightweight printed solar sheets are ideal for camping gear, emergency kits, and portable chargers where weight matters.
  • IoT (Internet of Things) and sensor-powered devices: Small-scale printed solar cells can power remote sensors and low-energy devices without needing battery replacements.
  • Space and disaster relief: CSIRO has even sent printed flexible solar modules into space aboard a private satellite, and researchers note their potential for rapid deployment in disaster recovery and defence situations.

Printable Solar vs Traditional Silicon Panels: Which One Should You Choose?

For home and rooftop solar installations, traditional rooftop solar panels remain the better choice today, offering higher efficiency, proven durability, and lower long-term costs. Printable solar panels are better suited to niche, flexible, or portable applications for now, not full home power needs.

Efficiency Comparison

Traditional silicon panels sit comfortably at 22.5%- 26% efficiency in real-world conditions. Printed perovskite modules have reached similar numbers in lab settings, but real-world, large-area printed modules still lag behind, with CSIRO’s own record-setting printed module reaching 11% efficiency at a practical scale.

Cost Comparison

While printable solar panels’ price per watt could eventually undercut silicon, thanks to cheaper materials and faster production, current estimates are still higher than mainstream silicon panel costs. Silicon has decades of manufacturing scale behind it, which keeps its price highly competitive.

Use-Case Comparison

Silicon panels are the right choice when you need long-term, high-output power for a home or business rooftop. Printable solar cells are ideal where weight, flexibility, or unconventional surfaces matter more than raw efficiency.

What’s Next for Printable Solar Panels

Printable solar technology is moving from research labs into pilot-scale manufacturing, with global research institutions racing to solve durability issues while pushing efficiency higher. The next five to ten years will likely decide whether this technology becomes a mainstream alternative or stays a specialised niche.

  • Current Research Trends

Government-backed research bodies like CSIRO (Australia) and NREL (United States) are leading much of this work. CSIRO’s Printed Photovoltaic Facility, a dedicated space for scaling up flexible solar cell production, represents a serious institutional bet on this technology’s future.

  • Realistic Timeline for Mainstream Adoption

Most experts and researchers agree that full commercial, home-scale adoption of printable solar panels is still some years away. Durability, at 25-year lifespans matching silicon, is the single biggest hurdle left to clear. For now, expect printable solar to grow steadily in niche and industrial applications before it challenges silicon panels at home.

Printable solar panels represent one of the most interesting shifts in solar technology in decades. By replacing rigid silicon wafers with printable inks, researchers have opened the door to solar cells that are thinner, lighter, and potentially far cheaper to produce at scale. What Is Solar Inverter Clipping?

A solar inverter converts the direct current (DC) electricity produced by solar panels into alternating current (AC) electricity for use in your home or export to the grid. Inverter clipping occurs when the panels can supply more power than the inverter can convert at that moment. The inverter then limits the power drawn from the panels to keep its AC output within its rated capacity.

Why Does Inverter Clipping Happen?

Inverter clipping happens when solar panels can supply more power than the inverter can convert into AC electricity at a given moment. Since the inverter has a fixed maximum output capacity, it limits the power drawn from the panels to stay within that capacity.

Several factors can increase the chances of inverter clipping:

  • Higher solar panel capacity: Installers often connect panels with a higher total rated capacity than the inverter’s AC rating because panels rarely operate at their full rated output.   • Strong sunlight: On clear, sunny days, panels may produce enough DC power to exceed the inverter’s conversion capacity, particularly around midday.
  • Cooler panel temperatures: Solar panels generally operate more efficiently at lower temperatures. Strong sunlight combined with cooler temperatures can increase power output and the chances of clipping.

The difference between panel capacity and inverter capacity is measured using the DC/AC ratio, also called the inverter loading ratio (ILR). A polycrystalline solar panel is a low-wattage photovoltaic module made using silicon cells whose wafers contain multiple crystal grains. These grains form when molten silicon cools and solidifies, giving polycrystalline cells their characteristic non-uniform crystal structure.

The terminology can be better understood via a simple flow, listed below:

How Are Polycrystalline Solar Cells Made?

Polycrystalline solar cells are made by melting high-purity silicon, allowing it to solidify into a multicrystalline ingot, slicing the ingot into wafers, and processing those wafers into photovoltaic cells.

Unlike monocrystalline silicon, the resulting material contains many crystal grains.

The process can be simplified into the following steps:

  1. High-purity polysilicon is produced from purified silicon feedstock.
  2. Polysilicon is melted at high temperatures.
  3. The molten silicon is cooled slowly from the bottom, allowing it to solidify gradually.
  4. Multiple silicon crystal grains form during solidification, creating a multicrystalline ingot.
  5. The ingot is cut into blocks and thin wafers.
  6. The wafers are textured and processed to form the electrical junction required for a solar cell.
  7. Anti-reflective coatings and electrical contacts are added.
  8. Individual cells are interconnected and encapsulated within a glass enclosure to form the finished solar module.

What is a 12-Volt DC Solar Panel?

A 12-volt DC solar panel is a photovoltaic module designed primarily for nominal 12V electrical systems. It converts sunlight into direct current (DC) electricity and is commonly paired with 12V batteries, solar charge controllers, DC loads, or small off-grid power systems.

The 12V rating alone does not tell you the panel’s wattage. A 12V panel could be rated at 20W, 50W, 100W, 150W, or another capacity depending on its design.

Does a 12V Solar Panel Actually Produce 12 Volts?

No, a 12-volt DC solar panel does not normally operate at exactly 12V. The 12V figure is a nominal system rating. Its maximum-power voltage and open-circuit voltage need to be higher to deliver charging power to a 12V battery through suitable charging electronics.

For example, a nominal-12V module can have a nominal voltage of 12V, but its voltage at maximum power (Vmp) can be 20V, and its open-circuit voltage (Voc) can be 22.50V

How Does a 12-Volt Solar Panel Work?

A 12V solar panel works by converting sunlight directly into DC electricity through photovoltaic cells. The electrical output can then power a compatible DC load or pass through a charge controller to recharge a battery.

Here is how the process works:

  1. Sunlight reaches the solar cells: Photons transfer energy to electrons inside the photovoltaic material.
  2. The cells generate DC electricity: The movement of these electrons creates electrical current.
  3. Connected cells provide usable voltage and current: The cell arrangement determines the panel’s electrical characteristics.
  4. A charge controller regulates battery charging: When a battery is in use, a compatible controller manages the current delivered to it.
  5. The battery stores energy: Stored solar electricity can supply power to loads when sunlight is low or absent.
  6. An inverter can supply AC loads: If the connected device requires AC (alternating current) rather than DC, a suitable inverter converts battery DC into AC.

Solar output changes with irradiance, shade, temperature, dust, and panel orientation. The wattage printed on a panel represents rated output under specified test conditions, not guaranteed output throughout the day.

12-volt PV panels are available in different cell technologies and physical designs, including monocrystalline, polycrystalline, flexible, and foldable modules. The right type depends on space, portability, durability, and the amount of power required.

  • Flexible 12V solar panels: Flexible PV panels feature a lightweight, bendable construction and suit curved surfaces on boats, vans, RVs, and similar mobile installations.
  • Portable/foldable 12V solar panels: These portable solar modules fold for transport and can be deployed temporarily during camping or other off-grid activities. Portable kits may also include a charge controller.

How Much Power Does a 12V Solar Panel Produce?

A 12V solar panel can produce very different amounts of power depending on its wattage and current rating. The 12V label does not determine its wattage.

Electrical power can be calculated using a simple formula listed below:

Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A)

For example, if a 12V panel has a maximum-power voltage (Vmp) of 20V and a maximum-power current (Imp) of 5A, then the power it delivers is 100W (20V Vmp × 5A Imp).

How Many Amps Does a 12V Solar Panel Produce?

The current produced by a 12V solar panel depends on its power rating (wattage) and maximum power voltage (Vmp). A higher-wattage panel will produce more current than a lower-wattage panel operating at a similar voltage.

You can estimate the current using the formula listed below:

Current (A) = Power (W) ÷ Voltage (V)

For example, if a 100W 12V solar panel has a Vmp of 20V, then 100W ÷ 20V = 5A.

So, the panel produces about 5A at its maximum power point.

The exact current should always be checked in the panel’s datasheet under Imp, or current at maximum power. Do not calculate it by simply dividing the wattage by the nominal 12V rating because the panel’s actual operating voltage is usually higher.

The panel current and the battery-charging current can also differ. An MPPT (maximum power point tracking) charge controller can convert the panel’s higher voltage into a lower charging voltage while increasing the available charging current.

Charging time depends on how much energy must be replaced and how much charging power actually reaches the battery.

Yes, a compatible solar charge controller is generally required when a 12V panel charges a rechargeable solar battery. It regulates charging and prevents the panel from simply feeding changing solar voltage directly into the battery.

  • Controls charging voltage and current: It keeps battery charging within the controller’s programmed limits.
  • Matches the charging setup to the battery: Battery chemistry and voltage must be supported by the controller.
  • PWM (pulse width modulation) vs MPPT (maximum power point tracking): PWM controllers are simpler, while MPPT controllers can convert excess panel voltage into useful charging current and usually offer greater flexibility.
  • Controller amperage: The controller must handle the expected charging current and remain within its specified PV input voltage, current, and wattage limits.

Which Details Should You Look for on All Four Bills?

What Changes in Your Electricity Bill After Installing a Solar System?

After you install rooftop solar and connect it to the grid, a bidirectional net meter or smart meter records electricity moving in both directions. Your bill shows the units imported from the grid and surplus solar units exported to it. The DISCOM adjusts these figures before calculating energy charges and solar-purchase credits.

  • How does a normal electricity meter work? A normal electricity meter records the units drawn from the grid. The DISCOM calculates the energy charges using these units and the tariff applicable to your consumer category.
  • How does a bidirectional solar meter work? A bidirectional net meter records two electricity flows. Import occurs when your home draws electricity from the grid. Export occurs when your solar panels generate more electricity than your home uses and send the surplus to the grid.

Why Your Solar App and Electricity Bill Show Different Units

Your inverter or solar-monitoring application generally displays total solar generation. The meter records only the electricity exchanged with the grid.

  • Some solar electricity is consumed instantly by appliances inside your home.
  • Since this electricity never reaches the grid, it does not appear as exported electricity.

Therefore, total solar generation = direct solar consumption + exported solar electricity

This distinction makes it easier to understand the detailed fields printed on the solar bill.

How to Read Each Section of an MGVCL, PGVCL, UGVCL, and DGVCL Solar Bill?

Before checking the solar calculation, confirm that the bill belongs to the correct consumer, meter, and billing period. A wrong meter number, tariff category, or reading period can affect every calculation that follows, even if the import-export formula itself appears correct.

Here are the most important sections of your solar bill.

  1. Bill date, due date, and billing period: The bill date is when the DISCOM generated the bill. The due date is the last date to pay the bill without incurring a delayed-payment charge. The billing period shows the dates covered by the bill. Check it before comparing two bills because one may cover more days than the other.
  2. Consumer number and consumer name: The consumer number uniquely identifies your electricity connection. You will need it to access your respective solar bill online, make a payment, or raise a complaint. Confirm that the consumer name and service address are correct.
  3. Meter number: Match the meter number printed on the bill with the number on the bidirectional meter installed at your premises. After a meter replacement, the bill may also contain closing readings from the old meter and opening readings from the new one.
  4. Tariff category and sanctioned load: The tariff category identifies the rates applicable to the connection. Sanctioned load is the maximum connected load approved by the DISCOM. It differs from your rooftop solar capacity and may affect fixed charges.
  5. Smart meter details: If a smart meter is installed, the bill may show its identification, reading date, and meter status.
  6. Maximum demand: Maximum demand is the highest load recorded during the billing period, generally shown in kW (kilowatt) or kVA (kilovolt-ampere). It is not the total electricity consumed and should not be confused with sanctioned load or solar generation.
  7. Previous and current import readings: Import readings are cumulative meter values. Check that both readings belong to the correct meter and billing period. The difference is used later to calculate the electricity imported during the current period.
  8. Previous and current export readings: Export readings are also cumulative. Confirm that the previous and current figures are recorded correctly. Their difference is used to calculate how much surplus solar electricity reached the grid during the billing period.
  9. Multiplying factor and recorded units: The multiplying factor (MF) converts the recorded difference into actual units. It may be shown as MF on the bill. For many residential connections, it is 1, but you should use the value printed on your bill.

Actual units = Meter-reading difference × multiplying factor

  1. Single-phase or three-phase connection: The phase field indicates whether the property has a single-phase or three-phase electricity meter supply. It describes the connection arrangement, not the number of panels or the amount of solar generation.

Now that each field is clear, you can use the import and export readings to understand the central solar calculation.

How to Read Import and Export Units on an MGVCL, PGVCL, UGVCL, and DGVCL Solar Bill?

Import units represent electricity drawn from the grid, while export units represent surplus solar electricity supplied to it. The current-period figures are calculated from the cumulative meter readings and the multiplying factor shown on the bill.

Use these formulas:

  • Current import units = (Current import reading − Previous import reading) × Multiplying factor
  • Current export units = (Current export reading − Previous export reading) × Multiplying factor

Once these values are known, compare them:

  • If import is higher, net imported units = Import units − Export units. These units remain billable under the applicable tariff.
  • If export is higher, excess export units = Export units − Import units. Eligible excess units are valued at the applicable Solar Purchase Rate.
  • If both are equal, there are no net imported or excess exported units for that period. However, fixed charges will still apply, along with any other applicable charges.

The unit adjustment should be completed before energy charges or solar-purchase credits are checked.

How Are Fixed Charges and Energy Charges Calculated?

After import and export units are adjusted, energy charges are calculated on the remaining billable units, where applicable. Fixed charges are linked to the electricity connection or sanctioned load and can remain payable even when solar export fully offsets grid import.

  • Energy charges apply when imported units remain after export unit adjustment.
  • The tariff depends on the consumer category, and domestic slab rates may apply to the remaining billable consumption.
  • If the tariff contains slabs, each portion of consumption is charged at its respective slab rate. You should not multiply every unit by the highest rate.

Other charges that may appear include:

  • Fuel and Power Purchase Price Adjustment
  • Electricity duty
  • Meter-related charges
  • Delayed-payment charges
  • Debit or credit adjustments
  • Rounding adjustment

If export exceeds import, the calculation moves in the opposite direction and creates a solar-purchase credit.

How Is the Solar Purchase Rate or PPA Credit Calculated?

When eligible solar export exceeds grid import, the excess units are purchased at the Solar Purchase Rate or PPA rate applicable to the connection.

  • The resulting amount is a monetary credit.
  • It may be adjusted against fixed charges, other bill components, or an outstanding balance.

What Is the Solar Purchase Rate?

The Solar Purchase Rate is the price the DISCOM pays for each eligible excess unit exported after import has been adjusted. The current rate is ₹2.25 per unit.

This is different from the retail tariff you pay for grid electricity. Therefore, excess export should not be valued using your domestic electricity slab rate.

How Is Solar-Purchase Credit Calculated? Worked Example of PPA Credit

Let us first consider a month in which the household uses more grid electricity than it exports.

Case 1: Import is higher than export

  • DISCOM import units = 600
  • DISCOM export units = 400
  • Net imported units = 600 − 400 = 200 units

The consumer is billed for 200 units under the applicable tariff.

Now consider a month in which the rooftop solar system sends more electricity to the grid than the household imports.

Case 2: Export is higher than import

  • DISCOM import units = 600
  • DISCOM export units = 700
  • Excess export units = 700 − 600 = 100 units
  • Solar-purchase credit = 100 × ₹2.25 = ₹225

The ₹225 is a credit, not the final bill amount. The DISCOM still needs to account for connection-related charges and previous balances.

How Are Credit Balance and Arrears Adjusted?

The credit and arrears section connects the current bill with previous bills. A previous monetary credit can reduce the present amount payable, while arrears increase it. The solar-purchase credit generated in the current bill may also be adjusted against applicable current charges.

The calculation can be understood in three stages:

  1. Current bill charges = Energy charges + Fixed charges + Other applicable charges
  2. Balance after solar credit = Current bill charges − Current solar-purchase credit
  3. Final amount payable = Balance after solar credit + Arrears − Previous monetary credit

For example, suppose current charges total ₹350 and the solar-purchase credit is ₹225. The remaining current balance is ₹125. If there is a previous credit of ₹50 and no arrear, the final payable amount becomes ₹75.

The result changes when the credit is larger than all the amounts charged.

What Happens If the Solar Credit Is Higher Than the Current Charges?

If the available solar credit exceeds the current charges and arrears, the consumer may have no amount to pay for that bill. The remaining sum can appear as a closing credit balance for adjustment, subject to the applicable billing arrangement.

For example, if current charges are ₹200 and the available monetary credit is ₹300, ₹200 can be adjusted against the charges. The account is then left with a ₹100 credit, which is carried forward to the next bill.

What Is a Hybrid Inverter?

A hybrid inverter is a solar inverter that’s built to manage power from three sources at once:

  1. Solar panels
  2. The electricity grid
  3. Battery

It converts the Direct Current (DC) electricity generated by your solar panels into Alternating Current (AC), which is what your home’s appliances actually run on.

What makes it hybrid is the built-in Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) charge controller and battery management logic in a single unit; normally you’d need a separate charge controller and inverter. Because of this, a hybrid inverter can be installed two ways:

  • With a battery: For backup power during outages and time-of-day energy shifting
  • Without a battery: Functioning close to a standard on-grid (grid-tied) solar inverter, but keeping a battery-ready slot for later

Can a Hybrid Solar Inverter Work Without a Battery?

Yes. Most hybrid solar inverters are designed to run out of the box in a battery-less, grid-tied configuration. In this mode, the solar hybrid inverter without a battery does three things:

  1. Converts DC power from your solar panels into usable AC power
  2. Sends that power straight to your home’s active loads (lights, fans, ACs, appliances) as it’s generated
  3. Exports any surplus that your home doesn’t use, in real time, to the grid through your net meter/smart meter

There’s no storage step in between. If your panels are generating 4 kW of units and your home is drawing 2 kW worth of units, the extra 2 kW goes out to the grid; it isn’t banked inside the inverter for later. However, some hybrid inverter systems can continue powering selected loads during a grid outage. What Are Solar Roofing Sheets?

Solar roofing sheets are roofing systems that generate electricity directly from sunlight while doubling as the roof itself, rather than sitting on top of an existing roof. Made from photovoltaic (PV) materials, they’re part of a broader category called Building Integrated Photovoltaics (BIPV), solar technology designed to replace conventional building materials like roof tiles, facades, or windows.

Types of Solar Roofing Sheets

Solar roofing sheets are typically built using semiconductor materials like monocrystalline silicon or copper-indium-gallium selenide (CIGS), and fall into a few broad categories:

1. Solar Tiles (Solar Shingles)

Designed to mimic the size and look of traditional roofing tiles, asphalt, slate, or terracotta; solar tiles blend into a conventional roofline while quietly generating power. They’re the closest thing to an “invisible” solar roof, which makes them popular for heritage-style or design-led homes.

2. Building-Integrated Solar Panels (BIPV Panels)

These have dimensions closer to standard solar panels and can be integrated into a roof or building facade. They’re less discreet than solar tiles but generate more power per square metre, and tend to be more cost-effective for larger roof areas.

3. Solar Metal Roofing

Thin-film or laminate PV cells integrated directly into metal roofing sheets (steel, aluminium, or copper). This option suits sheds, warehouses, and homes that already use metal roofing, combining the durability of metal with built-in solar generation.

4. Solar Skylights and PV Glass

Semi-transparent photovoltaic glass used for skylights or facades; these generate power while still allowing light through, mostly used in premium commercial or architect-led residential projects.

What Type of Roofing Is Best Under Solar Panels? 

If you’re installing standard rack-mounted solar panels rather than integrated sheets, roof type matters as much as roof direction. Here’s how common Indian roof types compare:

  • RCC (concrete) roofs: The easiest and most economical to install on; mounting structures anchor directly and securely, with no membrane to puncture.
  • Metal sheet roofs: Well-suited to solar, often using clamp-based mounting that avoids drilling altogether.
  • Asbestos roofs: Technically possible but not recommended; asbestos is brittle, prone to cracking under mounting loads, and carries health risks during drilling. Most installers recommend replacing asbestos roofing before adding solar.
  • Sloped tile/heritage roofs: Installable, but require careful waterproofing at each mounting point to avoid leaks; this is where solar tiles or BIPV panels often make more visual and structural sense than standard panels.

In short: a sound, flat RCC or metal roof gives you the simplest, most cost-effective solar installation. Solar roofing sheets become the more relevant option primarily for new builds, full re-roofs, or heritage-sensitive homes.

Yes, this is exactly what solar roofing sheets (solar tiles, BIPV panels, solar metal roofing) are built to do: they replace your roofing material rather than mount on top of it. However, this only works cleanly in two situations:

  1. New construction, where the solar roof is planned into the building design from the start.
  2. A full re-roof, where you’re replacing your roofing material anyway and can absorb solar into that cost.

For an existing home with a sound roof, standard rack-mounted solar panels installed on top of your current roof remain the far more practical and economical route; you don’t need to touch your existing roofing at all.

Benefits of Solar Roofing Sheets

Maximum sunlight exposure is one of the most significant benefits of installing solar roofs. This is helpful in generating sufficient amounts of electricity for domestic use.

Apart from this, there are other perks offered by this solar power generating system. These include:

  • Maximum sunlight exposure: Full roof coverage means efficient power generation for the space used.
  • Efficient protection: UV-resistant and typically fire-resistant, while keeping interiors cooler than a bare roof.
  • No separate roof + panel cost: Since the sheet is the roof, you avoid paying for both roofing material and mounted panels separately.
  • Preserves home aesthetics: Blends with the building rather than sitting visibly on top of it.
  • Durability: Built to withstand storms, wind, and debris, much like conventional roofing.

Disadvantages of Solar Roofing Sheets

Using solar on roofs is undoubtedly an excellent investment for reducing the harsh environmental impact of commercial power generation. However, they come with certain drawbacks.

You must consider the disadvantages of solar roofing sheets before installing them. Two major limitations of this system are listed below.

  • Higher cost per watt: Typically 2-3 times more expensive than standard panels for the same generation capacity.
  • Lower efficiency and limited availability in India: The market is still maturing, with far fewer installers and products compared to standard rooftop solar systems.
  • Best suited to new construction: Retrofitting an existing sound roof with solar sheets rarely makes financial sense compared to standard panels.

Conclusion

Solar roofing sheets solve a real problem, generating power without visible panels, but they solve it at a real cost. For most Indian homes with a sound existing roof, standard rack-mounted solar panels remain the far more practical choice: lower cost per watt, faster payback, wider subsidy eligibility, and a mature installer network to back it up. Solar roofing sheets earn their place mainly in new construction or a full re-roof, where design and aesthetics genuinely justify the premium.

Frequently Asked Questions 

What type of roofing is best under solar panels?

RCC (concrete) and metal sheet roofs are best suited for standard solar panel installation, they’re structurally simple to mount on and don’t require drilling into fragile material. Asbestos roofs should generally be replaced before installing solar.

Is it better to install solar tiles or rooftop solar panels on an existing home?

Rooftop solar panels are the better choice for nearly every existing home; they cost less per watt, generate more power, install without touching your existing roof, and come with full subsidy and net-metering support. Solar tiles are worth considering only if you’re building new or doing a full re-roof.

Can solar roofing sheets be installed on any type of roof?

Not easily. They’re primarily suited to new builds or full re-roofs, since installation typically replaces the existing roofing layer rather than sitting on top of it.

What are solar roofing sheets made of?

Solar roofing sheets are made from photovoltaic semiconductor materials like monocrystalline silicon or copper-indium-gallium selenide (CIGS), shaped to resemble traditional roofing tiles, shingles, or metal sheets.

Do solar roofing sheets need special maintenance?

They generally need the same upkeep as any roof: periodic cleaning and inspection, though their integrated design can make individual panel repairs more involved than with rack-mounted rooftop solar panels, where a single panel can be swapped out easily.
