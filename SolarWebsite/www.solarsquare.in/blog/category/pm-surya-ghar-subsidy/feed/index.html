]]> The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) is reportedly working on a redesigned version of India’s rooftop solar scheme, which media and installers are calling PM (Pradhan Mantri) Surya Ghar 2.0. The reported plan would reward the power a system actually generates, not just the capacity installed. It may also add support for battery storage and bring in households that can’t use the scheme today, such as apartment residents. None of this is confirmed policy yet, and it is still under discussion at the ministry.

This guide separates what has been notified from what is only being talked about, whether you search for PM Surya Ghar Yojana 2.0 or PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana 2.0. It covers the current subsidy, eligibility, the 2026 rule changes, and whether to wait.

What Is PM Surya Ghar 2.0?

PM Surya Ghar 2.0 (also searched as PM Surya Ghar Yojana 2.0 or PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana 2.0) is an informal label. It describes the possible next phase of India’s residential rooftop solar programme. It is not an official scheme name. Here is how that label came about, and how much of it is confirmed.

Where Things Stand Today

PM Surya Ghar 2.0 has not been officially launched. The original scheme began in February 2024 with an outlay of ₹75,021 crore, and it is meant to run until the end of the financial year (FY) 2026-27, as MNRE’s scheme guidelines and Press Information Bureau (PIB) material describe. Today the solar subsidy in India depends on installed capacity alone; batteries get no subsidy of their own, and apartment residents largely sit outside the scheme. The 2.0 talk began after MNRE reportedly held a brainstorming session on 23 July 2026 on the next roadmap. Trade media said a discussion document would follow, but nothing has been notified since, so treat every 2.0 detail as a proposal.

What Could PM Surya Ghar 2.0 Change?

The ideas reported so far fall into a few buckets. None of them is policy yet.

Subsidy basis Fixed amount by system size Part of the support linked to actual electricity generation

If you want proof that storage is already on MNRE’s radar, look at the scheme’s “Innovative Projects” component. It earmarks ₹500 crore for pilots such as rooftop solar with battery storage and electric vehicles, funding up to 60% of project cost or ₹30 crore, whichever is lower (All India Radio). That is a pilot window for startups and institutions, not a household subsidy.

PM Surya Ghar 2.0 Subsidy: What You Can Count On Today

People search “PM Surya Ghar 2.0 subsidy” hoping for a new number. There isn’t one. The numbers below apply to the current scheme and are in effect now.

Subsidy for Individual Homes

The central financial assistance (CFA) is calculated per kilowatt (kW) and capped at 3 kW, as listed on state government scheme pages and the national portal.

System size Central subsidy
1 kW ₹30,000
2 kW ₹ 60,000
3 kW & Above Capped at ₹ 78,000

So a 5 kW system gets the same central subsidy as a 3 kW one. The subsidy is credited by Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) to your bank account through the portal.

Subsidy for Housing Societies

Group Housing Societies (GHS) and Resident Welfare Associations (RWA) get ₹18,000 per kW for common facilities, including EV charging, up to 500 kW (calculated at 3 kW per house), as per official scheme listings.

Will Your Subsidy Change Under 2.0?

There’s no way to say yet. A generation-linked model could mean some of the support arrives over time instead of in one payment, but that is speculation until MNRE publishes a draft. Any state top-up is separate. States can add to the central amount; for example, Gujarat has newly reported support for systems up to 2.18 kWp from 22 October 2026, so always check your state nodal agency, too.

PM Surya Ghar Yojana 2.0 Eligibility: Who Can Apply?

No separate eligibility list exists for 2.0. What you can apply under today is the existing scheme, so here is who it covers and what the proposals might widen.

Eligibility Under the Current Scheme

According to MNRE’s reply in the Lok Sabha on 29 July 2026, all residential consumers with a grid-connected electricity connection from their local distribution company (DISCOM) can apply on the National Portal for PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana. In practice, you will need:

  • A residential electricity connection in your name
  • A rooftop with enough shadow-free space
  • A system installed by a vendor registered on the portal
  • Equipment that meets the scheme’s domestic content rules

Commercial and industrial buildings are not covered by the residential subsidy.

Could Eligibility Expand Under 2.0?

Possibly. Apartment residents and renters without their own roof are the obvious gap, which is why shared solar keeps coming up in the reports. Until MNRE says so officially, don’t assume you qualify through a route that doesn’t exist yet.

What Has Changed in PM Surya Ghar Rules in 2026?

While 2.0 stays a proposal, the existing scheme has been tightened and simplified. These are the changes that matter to a homeowner.

Easier Approvals and Cheaper Loans

MNRE’s Lok Sabha reply lists the measures taken to speed things up:

  • Collateral-free loans from nationalised banks at repo rate plus 50 basis points (5.75% a year at present) for 10 years
  • Technical feasibility requirement waived, with auto load enhancement up to 10 kW
  • Net metering agreement built into the portal application, so no separate paperwork
  • A grievance helpline, 15555, running in 12 languages

Equipment and Quality Rules

Domestic content matters. Under the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM) List-II, solar cells must be on the approved list for projects commissioned after 1 June 2026, as industry guides report. MNRE has also set a 15-day window for vendors to fix defects flagged in third-party inspections. In plain words, a poor installation can delay your subsidy.

Progress and Deadline

As of 27 July 2026, MNRE reported 40,45,298 systems installed, 48,80,796 households benefited, and ₹27,343.9 crore released as CFA. The official timeline still runs to FY 2026-27. Reports of an extension exist, but we found no notification.

Should You Wait for PM Surya Ghar 2.0?

For most households, no. The scheme you can use is open, funded, and paying out. Waiting means paying higher electricity bills each month for a scheme with no date. A few things worth weighing:

  • Timeline risk: The current scheme is approved only till FY 2026-27, and the subsidy is released after inspection, not at application.
  • Battery plans: If you want storage, you can add a battery privately today, though it is not subsidised under the current scheme.
  • Apartment dwellers: You may be better off watching for official shared-solar rules, or checking whether your society can apply as a GHS or RWA.

The Bottom Line: Start With the Scheme That’s Live

PM Surya Ghar 2.0 is, for now, a reported idea with no notification, no subsidy amount, and no launch date. The scheme that pays today is the existing one: up to ₹ 78,000 in central subsidy, collateral-free loans, simpler approvals, and a fixed window that runs to 31 March 2027. Generation-linked incentives, battery support and shared solar may arrive later. Until MNRE notifies them, waiting only means paying full electricity bills while a working scheme sits unused.

So go for the current scheme, which has already benefited so many Indian households. What Is the Odisha Solar Subsidy?

There isn’t one single “Odisha subsidy”. What households call the solar subsidy in Odisha is really two layers of financial support stacked together: a central one from the Government of India and a top-up from the state. This section explains each layer so you know where the money comes from.

Central Subsidy Under PM Surya Ghar

Launched with an outlay of ₹75,021 crore, PM Surya Ghar aims to put rooftop solar on one crore homes by financial year 2026-27. The money you receive is called Central Financial Assistance (CFA). It is a fixed amount linked to your system size, not a percentage of what you pay your installer. Applications and tracking all happen on the official PM Surya Ghar portal, and the scheme rules sit with MNRE.

Odisha Govt Solar Subsidy (State Top-Up)

Odisha added its own support on top of the central amount. The state set aside ₹495 crore for this top-up in its financial year 2026-27 budget, routed through the Odisha Akshaya Shakti Bikash Yojana (OASBY).

A quick word of caution. Some national websites still say Odisha offers no additional state subsidy. Those pages were last updated in late 2025 and haven’t caught up. For the latest position, check with the Odisha Renewable Energy Development Agency (OREDA), which is the state nodal agency for rooftop solar.

What If You Install More Than 3 kW?

You can install a system above 3kW. The subsidy simply stops growing. Bigger systems still make sense if your consumption is high, but plan for the extra cost to come out of your own pocket.

Who Is Eligible for the Solar Panel Subsidy in Odisha?

Not everyone who installs solar gets the subsidy, so it pays to check the rules first. The following are the eligibility requirements for the solar panel subsidy in Odisha:

  • You are a residential consumer with a valid electricity connection from an Odisha distribution company (DISCOM).
  • You have a suitable, shadow-free roof that you own or have the right to use.
  • You install a grid-connected rooftop system.
  • Your installer is empanelled on the national portal.
  • Your panels and inverter meet the approved list and BIS (Bureau of Indian Standards) requirements.
  • You haven’t already claimed a rooftop solar subsidy for the same connection.

The four DISCOMs in Odisha are TPCODL (Tata Power Central Odisha Distribution Limited), TPNODL (Tata Power Northern Odisha Distribution Limited), TPSODL (Tata Power Southern Odisha Distribution Limited), and TPWODL (Tata Power Western Odisha Distribution Limited). Which one you deal with depends on your district.

Odisha Solar Subsidy Process: Step by Step

The application runs online, but several steps depend on your DISCOM and installer. This section walks through the journey in order, so you know what happens next at every stage.

Step 1: Register on the National Portal

Go to pmsuryaghar.gov.in, choose Odisha as your state, pick your DISCOM, and enter your consumer number. Register before you talk to any installer so the application is in your name from day one.

Step 2: Apply for Rooftop Solar

Log in with your consumer number and mobile number, then submit the rooftop solar application. Pick the system capacity based on your bills and the roof space you have.

Step 3: Wait for DISCOM Feasibility Approval

Your DISCOM checks whether your connection can take the system. Reports from installers suggest this stage commonly takes a couple of weeks, though it varies. According to the information published for Odisha, a separate technical feasibility study isn’t required for systems up to 10 kW.

Step 4: Choose an Empanelled Vendor

Pick an installer from the portal’s state-wise vendor list. Check the empanelment on the portal itself, not just on a brochure. Compare at least two or three quotes on panel brand, inverter, warranty, and after-sales support.

Step 5: Installation

The vendor surveys your roof, designs the system, and installs it. The physical installation of a home system often takes only a day or a few days. Scheduling and materials are usually what take longer.

Step 6: Net Meter Installation and Inspection

Your DISCOM installs a net meter, which records both what you draw from the grid and what you send back. It then inspects the system and issues a commissioning report. This is the step where delays most often creep in.

Step 7: Submit Bank Details

Once the system is commissioned, upload your bank details and a cancelled cheque on the portal.

Step 8: Receive the Subsidy

The central subsidy is credited to your bank account through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT). Ask your installer or OREDA how the Odisha state top-up is claimed and credited against your application, as the procedure can differ from the central payment.

Net Metering in Odisha

Net metering is what turns your rooftop plant from a power source into a money saver. This section explains how it works and what the rules allow.

With net metering, any extra electricity your panels produce during the day goes back to the grid. Your DISCOM credits those units against your consumption, so your bill falls. Odisha’s net metering framework, set under the Odisha Electricity Regulatory Commission (OERC), covers systems from 1 kWp to 500 kWp. Your DISCOM handles the meter installation and inspection.

  • For the first 2 kWp, this benchmark is ₹50,000 per kWp. This means the government considers a 1 kWp system at ₹50,000 and a 2 kWp system at ₹1,00,000 for subsidy calculation purposes.
  • This benchmark is not the same as the actual price quoted by a solar installer. Your installation may cost more or less depending on the panels, inverter, mounting structure, roof conditions, city, and other project requirements.

1. Unclear bank documents

Fix: Upload properly scanned, high-quality documents, not phone photos taken at an angle.

2. Name mismatch across documents

The name on your electricity bill and your bank account should match exactly. “R.P. Verma” on the bill versus “Ram Pratap Verma” on the bank account is enough to trigger a correction request, even though it’s clearly the same person.

Fix: Make sure both documents use the same name format before you submit.

3. Document and form mismatch

Bank details are typed in manually during the application, and everything you enter must exactly match the documents you upload: account number, account holder name, and IFSC (Indian Financial System Code) code. A single wrong digit in the account number can delay payment, route the subsidy to the wrong account, or trigger a recovery process that’s difficult to reverse. Fix: Cross-check the form against your uploaded documents line by line before you submit. Ideally, have a second person read the account number back to you before you hit the submit button.

After installation, your vendor uploads the Work Completion Report (WCR) and the Domestic Content Requirement (DCR) Certificate. The module serial numbers on the DCR certificate must exactly match the numbers entered on the portal. A mismatch can look like a duplicate entry to the system and trigger rejection.

Fix: Ask your installer to verify every module serial number against the portal entry before it’s uploaded.

5. Using the wrong bank account type

The subsidy can only be credited to an active savings account. Applications linked to a Current Account, NRE (Non-Resident External) Account, PPF (Public Provident Fund) Account, Loan Account, or Cash Credit Account can be rejected outright.

Fix: Always provide an active savings account in your name.

6. Handwritten bank documents

Documents with handwritten corrections, a handwritten account number, pen corrections, or a manually written IFSC code are not accepted.

Fix: Upload only machine-printed, original bank documents; request a fresh printed copy from your bank if needed.

The subsidy is only released for systems installed by an MNRE (Ministry of New and Renewable Energy)-empanelled vendor. If you hire a local electrician or an unlisted installer because they quoted a lower price, the application gets rejected once verification catches it, and by then, the panels are already on your roof.

Fix: Get the undertaking or NOC from the builder or the society’s managing committee in writing before you apply. In a society, this usually means a resolution passed at a committee meeting, so start the process early.

20. Geo-tagged photo requirement

Installation photographs uploaded after commissioning need to carry geo-tagging, showing the location coordinates of the installed system. Photos taken on a camera without location services enabled, images cropped or edited after capture, or screenshots of photos can strip out this data and delay the claim.

Fix: Ask your installer to capture the photographs using a geo-tagging camera app with location enabled, and to upload the original files without editing or compressing them.

Permanent vs Fixable Rejections

Not every rejection is the end of the road. Knowing which bucket your case falls into saves you time and false hope.

Permanent (cannot be reversed after the fact):

  1. Non-empanelled vendor: Any installation done by a vendor who is not registered on the PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana portal will not be eligible for solar subsidy and, the subsidy for that property is permanently lost. The system may still function fine, but the solar subsidy is gone.
  2. Non-ALMM-listed panels: If the panels installed aren’t on the approved list, there’s no way to retroactively qualify that installation for CFA (Central Financial Assistance).
  3. DCR non-compliance: If the cells/modules installed don’t meet the applicable Domestic Content Requirement, including the ALMM List-II requirement for cells, the installation cannot be retroactively made compliant. Verification has to happen before installation.

Fixable (before or after rejection):

  1. Bank document issues (blurry, handwritten, wrong account type, invalid document type): Correctable by uploading fresh, machine-printed documents from an active savings account.
  2. Name, account number, or IFSC mismatches between the form and your documents: Correctable by updating records and resubmitting.
  3. PFMS name mismatch: Fixable by confirming the exact name held in your bank’s core banking records and entering that version, or by getting the bank record itself updated.
  4. Bank account validation pending or rejected: Fixable by activating a dormant account, completing re-KYC, or clearing any restriction with your bank, then resubmitting.
  5. Bank name or IFSC changed after a merger: Fixable by obtaining the revised IFSC from your bank and uploading a fresh cheque leaf or passbook page.
  6. Missing undertaking form or government ID proof: Fixable by downloading the current undertaking format, completing and signing it, and uploading it with a clear ID scan.
  7. Builder, developer, or promoter undertaking not provided: Fixable by obtaining written consent from the builder or the society’s managing committee. Arrange this early, as society approvals can take time.
  8. WCR/DCR certificate mismatches: Fixable by having the vendor re-verify and re-upload the correct serial numbers.
  9. Geo-tagged photo requirement not met: Fixable by having the installer recapture the photographs with location services enabled and upload the unedited original files.
  10. Wrong connection category (commercial/industrial/agricultural instead of residential): Fixable by getting the connection category corrected with your DISCOM, then reapplying.
  11. Incorrect consumer number or DISCOM selected: Fixable by re-entering the correct consumer number and DISCOM as printed on your electricity bill.
  12. Incomplete installation or commissioning details: Fixable by having the vendor upload the missing certificates, correct equipment details, or clearer photographs.
  13. Pending net meter or commissioning: Not a rejection at all; resolves once the pending step (inspection, meter replacement, commissioning, vendor upload, etc.) is completed.
  14. Flagged as duplicate or previously suspended: Fixable by getting any prior application against the same consumer number or account formally closed, then reapplying.

The golden rule: All three permanent rejection causes, which relate to your vendor and the equipment they install, are also 100% preventable before installation begins. They’re never discovered by accident. Verifying the vendor’s empanelment, the panel model, and DCR/ALMM compliance in writing at the start prevents every one of them.

What Is the PM Kusum Yojana (PM Kusum Yojana Kya Hai)?

The PM Kusum Yojana is a central government scheme that promotes the use of solar energy in agriculture. It is implemented by the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE).

The scheme is associated with subsidized solar water pumps, but its scope is much wider.

It supports farmers in three main ways:

  1. Setting up small solar power plants on agricultural or barren land
  2. Installing standalone solar pumps in areas without reliable grid electricity
  3. Solarizing existing grid-connected agricultural pumps and feeders

Main Objectives of the PM Kusum Yojana

The scheme allows farmers to access a more reliable source of electricity for irrigation, reduce their dependence on diesel, and, in some cases, earn additional income by supplying surplus solar power to the grid.

Here are its main objectives:

  • Improve energy security for farmers
  • Provide a dependable source of power for irrigation
  • Reduce the use of diesel-powered agricultural pumps
  • Lower the cost of running irrigation systems
  • Help farmers earn from surplus solar electricity
  • Reduce pollution and promote clean energy in agriculture

In simple terms, the goal of PM Kusum Yojana is to give farmers a stable irrigation power supply while reducing their energy expenses.

Is PM Kusum the Same as PM-Kisan?

No, PM Kusum and PM-Kisan are two separate government schemes. Many times, people use the PM kisan kusum Yojana wrongly. In reality, both the schemes are different.

  • PM Kusum Solar Yojana: It supports solar power plants, solar pumps, and the solarization of agricultural electricity systems.
  • PM-Kisan Yojana: It provides direct income support to eligible farming families.

Enrolling in one scheme does not automatically register a farmer for the other.

Net Metering and Billing Under the Soura Rooftop Solar Scheme

Solar net metering is the billing system that converts your rooftop solar generation into savings. It tracks how much solar power your home uses directly, how much extra power you send to the KSEB grid, and how that exported power is adjusted against your bill.

How Does Net Metering Work?

Here’s the full flow for net metering in Kerala:

  1. Solar power is used by your home: If your rooftop PV system is generating electricity while your appliances are running, that solar power is used directly, reducing the units you buy from KSEB.
  2. Extra solar power is exported to the grid: If the system produces more electricity than your home needs at that moment, the extra units are sent to the KSEB grid.
  3. Exported units are adjusted against imported units: KSEB first adjusts the exported solar units against the electricity you import during the same billing cycle.
  4. Unused surplus is banked for later use: If any exported units remain after adjustment, they are banked. These banked units can be used to offset your future electricity imports.
  5. Banked units are used in a set order: When you use banked energy later, it is first adjusted against solar-hour imports, then off-peak imports, and then peak-hour imports.
  6. Some surplus can be used to cover fixed charges: If surplus units are still available at the end of the billing period, the units needed to cover fixed charges, meter rent, and similar charges are settled at the applicable APPC or feed-in tariff rate.
  7. Year-end surplus is settled separately: At the end of the financial year, any remaining banked energy is paid out by KSEB before 30 April of the following year, unless you choose to carry it forward.

KSERC Settlement: Banking and What KSEB Pays for Surplus?

If KSEB settles your remaining surplus in cash, the rate depends on whether you are an existing or new prosumer (producer and consumer of solar electricity):

  • Existing prosumers: ₹ 3.08 per kWh
  • New prosumers: ₹ 2.79 per kWh for solar

Net metering necessarily does not mean KSEB pays you the full retail electricity tariff for every extra unit you export. Your biggest savings come from using your own solar power and offsetting exported units against your bill. Cash payout applies only to the surplus remaining after billing and banking adjustments.
