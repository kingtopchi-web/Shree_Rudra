]]> Did you know that scientists can now print solar cells the same way a newspaper is printed? Australia’s national science agency, CSIRO (Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation), has even built a dedicated printed photovoltaic facility to do exactly this, rolling out solar cells onto thin plastic sheets.

Printable solar panels are thin, bendable solar cells made by printing a special solar ink onto plastic or film, rather than cutting them from silicon wafers. They can be produced on rolls, much like newspapers or banknotes, making them light, bendable, and cheaper to manufacture at scale.

In this blog, we will break down what printable solar panels actually are, how they are made, where they are used, their pros and cons, and where this technology is headed next. Whether you are a curious homeowner or someone tracking the future of solar energy in India, this guide will give you a clear, honest picture.

What Are Printable Solar Panels?

Printable solar panels are solar cells made by printing light-absorbing materials, like organic compounds or perovskites, onto thin, flexible surfaces like plastic film. Instead of slicing silicon into rigid wafers, manufacturers dissolve special solar materials into an ink and print them directly onto a surface.

Printed solar panels are very different from how traditional solar panels are made. A standard silicon solar panel starts with a silicon ingot, sliced into thin wafers, processed, and assembled into a rigid glass-covered panel. It is a heavy, multi-step industrial process.

Printable solar cells replace silicon wafers with a printable “solar ink.” This ink is made from light-sensitive compounds and can be applied by standard printing techniques. As a result, printable solar panels are lightweight sheets that can bend, curve, and be rolled up.

Because the core function remains the same as any solar cell, capturing sunlight and converting it into electricity, this technology is sometimes called printable photovoltaic cells. It’s just the manufacturing process that changes.

How Do Printable Solar Panels Work?

Printable solar panels work by using solar inks made from light-absorbing materials. These materials are deposited layer-by-layer onto flexible film using techniques like inkjet, slot-die, or screen printing. Once printed, these layers form a working solar cell that generates electricity when exposed to sunlight.

Printable solar panels are made by using materials like:

  • Organic photovoltaics (OPV): Organic photovoltaic cells are carbon-based polymer-fullerene compounds that can be dissolved into inks and printed onto flexible sheets.
  • Perovskites: Perovskites are crystal-structured materials that have seen the fastest efficiency growth of any solar technology in history. These are synthetic or mixed organic-inorganic structures that can be dissolved into liquid inks. According to the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), perovskite solar cells improved from around 3% efficiency in 2009 to over 25% within roughly a decade.

Source: NREL

The Printing Process of Print Solar Panels

The printing process of print solar panels includes ink preparation, plastic films, printing through industrial printing techniques, layering, sealing, and then testing.

  1. Ink preparation: The active solar material (organic compound or perovskite) is dissolved into a liquid ink.
  2. Substrate loading: A long roll of thin plastic film is fed into the printing system.
  3. Roll-to-roll printing: The ink is applied onto the moving film using industrial printing techniques, similar to how newspapers are printed at scale.
  4. Layering: Additional functional layers (electrodes, protective coatings) are printed or deposited on top.
  5. Curing and sealing: The printed sheet is dried and sealed to protect it from moisture and air.
  6. Testing: Each printed section is tested for performance before being cut and packaged.

CSIRO researchers built a system capable of manufacturing and testing more than 10,000 printed solar cells a day, which helped them quickly identify the best settings for large-scale roll-to-roll production. This is the scale and speed that makes printable solar panels commercially exciting.

Types of Printable Solar Technology

OPV cells use carbon-based, plastic-like compounds. They are extremely lightweight, low-cost to produce, and can even be made semi-transparent, useful for windows and building facades. Their efficiency, however, remains lower than other options.

Perovskites are currently the fastest-improving printable solar technology. CSIRO’s roll-to-roll printed perovskite modules achieved 15.5% efficiency for small cells and 11% for a 50 cm² printed module, a record for fully printed solar cells at that scale. In lab conditions, NREL has certified individual perovskite cells above 25% efficiency, though these are not yet commercial products.

Advantages of Printable Solar Panels

Printable solar panels offer several real advantages over traditional rigid panels, especially around cost, weight, and manufacturing speed.

  • Low manufacturing cost: Printing eliminates the need for silicon wafer processing, which lowers material and production costs. Researchers estimate that once roll-to-roll perovskite production is optimised, costs could fall per watt, still more than silicon, but a significant step toward affordability at scale.
  • Lightweight and flexible: Because they are printed on thin plastic film rather than glass, these panels can bend around curved surfaces.
  • Faster, scalable production: Roll-to-roll printing works much like printing newspapers, allowing continuous, high-speed manufacturing rather than the slower, batch-based process used for silicon panels.
  • New use cases: Their flexibility opens doors that rigid silicon panels simply cannot, curved rooftops, wearable devices, and semi-transparent windows, to name a few.

Limitations and Challenges of Printable Solar Panels

The biggest challenge with printable solar panels right now is durability, not performance. While printable solar panels’ efficiency has improved rapidly in the lab, most printed cells still degrade faster than silicon when exposed to real-world heat, humidity, and sunlight over years.

  • Lower efficiency compared to silicon: Most commercial printed solar cells still fall short of the efficiency that mainstream silicon panels offer today.
  • Durability and lifespan concerns: Silicon panels routinely last ~25 years or more. Printed perovskite and organic cells, by contrast, have historically lasted only months. Though the lifespan of printed solar panels can be improved with better sealing and materials.
  • Moisture and UV degradation: Because these cells are thinner and often lack the protective glass layer of traditional panels, they are more vulnerable to moisture ingress and UV damage over time.
  • Commercial scalability still developing: Scaling from lab success to a stable, mass-market solar panel printout remains a work in progress.

Real-World Applications of Printable Solar Panels

Even at this early stage, printable solar cells are already being tested and used in situations where traditional panels simply cannot go.

  • Building-integrated solar: Semi-transparent organic photovoltaic cells can be integrated into windows and facades, generating power without blocking light entirely.
  • Wearable electronics: Thin, flexible printable photovoltaic cells can be woven into clothing or accessories to power small electronics on the move.
  • Portable and off-grid chargers: Lightweight printed solar sheets are ideal for camping gear, emergency kits, and portable chargers where weight matters.
  • IoT (Internet of Things) and sensor-powered devices: Small-scale printed solar cells can power remote sensors and low-energy devices without needing battery replacements.
  • Space and disaster relief: CSIRO has even sent printed flexible solar modules into space aboard a private satellite, and researchers note their potential for rapid deployment in disaster recovery and defence situations.

Printable Solar vs Traditional Silicon Panels: Which One Should You Choose?

For home and rooftop solar installations, traditional rooftop solar panels remain the better choice today, offering higher efficiency, proven durability, and lower long-term costs. Printable solar panels are better suited to niche, flexible, or portable applications for now, not full home power needs.

Printed perovskite modules have reached similar numbers in lab settings, but real-world, large-area printed modules still lag behind, with CSIRO’s own record-setting printed module reaching 11% efficiency at a practical scale.

Cost Comparison

While printable solar panels’ price per watt could eventually undercut silicon, thanks to cheaper materials and faster production, current estimates are still higher than mainstream silicon panel costs. Silicon has decades of manufacturing scale behind it, which keeps its price highly competitive.

Use-Case Comparison

Silicon panels are the right choice when you need long-term, high-output power for a home or business rooftop. Printable solar cells are ideal where weight, flexibility, or unconventional surfaces matter more than raw efficiency.

]]> A conventional solar panel produces DC (direct current) electricity and sends it to a separate inverter. An AC solar module (ACM) takes a completely different approach. It has a microinverter factory-integrated into the module assembly at the back of the panel. So, DC-to-AC conversion happens at the panel itself.

Now, before you ask us if AC solar panels produce alternating current at the cell level, the answer is no. Solar cells in solar panels convert solar radiation into direct current (DC). In ACMs, since the microinverter is integrated into the panel’s frame, it converts the DC to AC on the spot.

However, AC solar modules remain uncommon in residential rooftop household solar panel systems in India. The major reason is their cost. Installing inverter electronics at every panel makes them considerably more expensive than conventional high-efficiency DC modules used with a string inverter.

This guide explains how AC solar panels work, their benefits and limitations, where they are used, and how they compare with conventional DC solar panels.

What are AC Solar Modules (ACMs)?

AC solar modules, also known as ACMs, are factory-made solar modules with a microinverter integrated into the module assembly. The microinverter is fitted to the rear of the panel as part of the complete product before it reaches the installation site.

This is an important distinction.

  • A true ACM is sold as an integrated panel-and-microinverter unit.
  • It differs from buying a standard DC solar panel and attaching a separately purchased microinverter during installation.

Do AC Solar Panels Actually Generate AC Electricity?

No. The photovoltaic cells inside AC solar panels still generate DC electricity.

The integrated microinverter converts that DC electricity into alternating current (AC) immediately at the module level. Solar photovoltaic technology itself produces DC electricity, regardless of the type of inverter used.

The term AC module refers to the electrical output of the complete factory-integrated unit. It does not mean the solar cells themselves generate AC.

What Components Are Built Into an AC Solar Module?

An AC solar module combines the following components into one module-level system:

  • PV (photovoltaic) cells: They generate DC electricity from sunlight.
  • Integrated microinverter: It converts the module’s DC output into AC.
  • AC cabling and connectors: They carry AC electricity from each module into the solar system.
  • Monitoring electronics: They allow module-level generation and performance monitoring, where the product supports it.

The defining component is the factory-integrated microinverter. Without it, the product is simply a conventional DC solar module.

How Do AC Solar Panels Work?

AC solar panels convert solar energy into grid-compatible AC electricity independently at every module. Each panel has its own factory-fitted microinverter. So the solar array does not rely on a single string inverter for DC-to-AC conversion.

Here’s how the process looks:

  1. Sunlight reaches the solar cells, where it is converted into DC electricity.
  2. The DC electricity travels a very short distance to the microinverter integrated into that module.
  3. The microinverter converts DC into AC electricity.
  4. AC output from multiple modules is combined via the system’s AC wiring.

AC Solar Module vs Solar Panel With a Microinverter

A true AC solar module and a DC panel connected to a microinverter can work in a similar way electrically. The difference is how the equipment is manufactured and supplied.

In AMCs, the microinverter is already integrated into the module assembly. In a solar panel with a microinverter, the microinverter is attached during system installation.

  • Factory-integrated ACM: Panel and microinverter come together as a single factory-made module.
  • Standard DC module + microinverter: Panel and microinverter are purchased separately and connected by the installer during installation.

What are the Benefits of AC Solar Modules?

The main benefits of AC solar modules stem from performing power conversion independently at every panel.

Here are all the advantages of AC solar panels:

  • Panel-level power conversion: Each module converts its own DC output into AC.
  • Better response to partial shading: A shaded module has less direct impact on the output of other modules.
  • Independent MPPT (maximum power point tracking): Each microinverter can track the maximum power point of its own panel.
  • Less high-voltage DC cabling: Conversion takes place at the module rather than after a long DC string.
  • No string inverter dependency: A problem with one microinverter affects only the associated module, rather than stopping solar generation across the entire array.

What are the Drawbacks of AC Solar Panels?

With so many benefits at hand, you are right to ask why they are not that common in rooftop solar systems in India. The reason is that they are extremely expensive. The ROI from DC modules with a string inverter is far higher than that of AC solar modules.

Here are all the drawbacks of AC solar panels:

  • Higher upfront cost: Every panel requires its own inverter electronics. In fact, the NREL (National Renewable Energy Laboratory) classifies microinverter systems as having a higher relative product price compared with string inverter systems.
  • More rooftop electronics: Every module has an electronic power-conversion device exposed to rooftop operating conditions.
  • Difficult repairs: Replacing a failed microinverter in an AC current solar panel requires lifting the associated panel.
  • Possible inverter clipping: The panel may occasionally produce more DC power than its integrated microinverter can convert.
  • Limited availability of factory-integrated ACM: Conventional DC modules are much more prevalent in the Indian solar market.
  • Less flexibility in component selection: The panel and inverter are already paired in a factory-integrated ACM.
  • Backup still needs a suitable system: Having AC output at each panel does not automatically provide electricity during a grid outage. If you need backup during outages, you will still need a battery energy storage system.

AC vs DC Solar Panels: What’s the Difference?

The core difference is where DC-to-AC conversion occurs. DC solar panels send DC power to a separate inverter, while AC solar modules convert it at each panel through a factory-integrated microinverter.

For Indian homes, DC solar panels are the better choice because they cost significantly less, require fewer rooftop electronics, and work efficiently with a string inverter on simple, shade-free roofs.

Here’s a snapshot of how the two differ across multiple parameters:

  • Power conversion: DC solar panels feed their power to a separate string inverter, which converts it to AC for the entire connected array. AC solar modules have a microinverter factory-integrated into each panel, so conversion occurs at the module level.
  • System cost: DC solar modules are more affordable because several panels share one inverter. ACMs, on the other hand, require inverter electronics at every module, which makes the overall rooftop solar system extremely expensive.
  • Roof suitability: DC panels work particularly well on roofs where panels face the same direction and receive uniform sunlight, which is usually abundant in India. ACMs can be useful for roofs with multiple orientations, slopes, or recurring partial shading.

How Much Do AC Solar Modules Cost?

Factory-built AC solar modules cost considerably more than conventional DC panels because every module includes its own microinverter. The final cost depends on the panel capacity, microinverter specification, monitoring equipment, installation, and warranty.

Although price varies, a ballpark range for a single ACM panel could be between ~₹25,000* and ~₹90,000*.

Are Polycrystalline Solar Panels Still Used for Rooftop Solar in 2026?

Polycrystalline technology now represents only a small part of India’s solar manufacturing capacity. Recent capacity additions have concentrated on TOPCon, HJT (Heterojunction Technology), and other monocrystalline technologies rather than conventional polycrystalline modules.

India’s latest solar manufacturing data shows how sharply this transition has happened.

The technology split was heavily tilted away from polycrystalline:

  • Monocrystalline technology accounted for more than 25%.
  • Polycrystalline modules accounted for only ~2%.

This difference was obvious at the cell level, too:

  • Monocrystalline solar cells represented more than 57% of manufacturing capacity.

  • Polycrystalline solar cells were down to ~3.5%.

The direction of new investment makes the change even clearer.

The absence of polycrystalline technology from major capacity additions indicates that solar panel manufacturers are prioritizing mono-based products for future demand. This reduces the availability of new, high-wattage polycrystalline modules in the Indian market.

Existing polycrystalline rooftop solar systems are not automatically obsolete. A system that remains safe and functional can continue generating electricity. The change is mainly relevant when deciding what solar panel technology to buy for a new rooftop solar plant in 2026.

What Are Polycrystalline Solar Panels Used For Today?

Polycrystalline silicon solar panels are now more relevant for small off-grid applications, specialized low-wattage needs, and as compatible replacements for older solar systems.

Current uses include:

  • Small battery-charging systems: Low-wattage polycrystalline modules can still charge batteries used for basic off-grid applications.
  • Solar lighting: Small modules remain suitable for lanterns, garden lights, and similar loads.
  • Camping and portable power: Compact poly panels are still sold for low-power portable applications.
  • Street lights and remote equipment: These systems may need only a small solar module rather than a high-efficiency residential panel.
  • IoT (Internet of Things) and monitoring systems: Remote sensors and communication equipment can operate from relatively small solar modules.
  • Older solar installations: A compatible polycrystalline solar module may be useful when replacing a damaged panel in an existing poly solar array.

What Is a Polycrystalline Solar Panel?

A polycrystalline solar panel is a low-wattage photovoltaic module made using silicon cells whose wafers contain multiple crystal grains. These grains form when molten silicon cools and solidifies, giving polycrystalline cells their characteristic non-uniform crystal structure.

Like other PV panels, polycrystalline panels convert solar radiation into DC (direct current) electricity through the photovoltaic effect. Sunlight absorbed by the semiconductor generates electrical charges that move through the cell and are collected by electrical contacts.

What Are Polycrystalline Solar Cells?

Polycrystalline solar cells are individual photovoltaic cells made from silicon wafers containing multiple crystal grains. Several cells are electrically connected and protected inside glass, encapsulant, and other materials to form a polycrystalline solar module or panel.

The terminology can be better understood via a simple flow, listed below:

  • Solar cell: The individual semiconductor device that converts sunlight into electricity.
  • Solar module or panel: Multiple connected solar cells packaged together.
  • Solar array: Multiple solar modules connected to form a larger PV system.

How Are Polycrystalline Solar Cells Made?

Polycrystalline solar cells are made by melting high-purity silicon, allowing it to solidify into a multicrystalline ingot, slicing the ingot into wafers, and processing those wafers into photovoltaic cells.

Unlike monocrystalline silicon, the resulting material contains many crystal grains.

The process can be simplified into the following steps:

  1. High-purity polysilicon is produced from purified silicon feedstock.
  2. Polysilicon is melted at high temperatures.
  3. The molten silicon is cooled slowly from the bottom, allowing it to solidify gradually.
  4. Multiple silicon crystal grains form during solidification, creating a multicrystalline ingot.
  5. The ingot is cut into blocks and thin wafers.
  6. The wafers are textured and processed to form the electrical junction required for a solar cell.
  7. Anti-reflective coatings and electrical contacts are added.
  8. Individual cells are interconnected and encapsulated within a glass enclosure to form the finished solar module.

Polycrystalline Silicon vs Polysilicon: Are They the Same?

No, they’re not the same thing.

  • Polysilicon is the high-purity silicon raw material used further upstream in solar manufacturing.
  • Polycrystalline or multicrystalline describes silicon containing multiple crystal grains.

This distinction is important because polysilicon raw material can also be used to make monocrystalline solar cells.

What is a 12-Volt DC Solar Panel?

A 12-volt DC solar panel is a photovoltaic module designed primarily for nominal 12V electrical systems. It converts sunlight into direct current (DC) electricity and is commonly paired with 12V batteries, solar charge controllers, DC loads, or small off-grid power systems.

The 12V rating alone does not tell you the panel’s wattage. A 12V panel could be rated at 20W, 50W, 100W, 150W, or another capacity depending on its design.

Does a 12V Solar Panel Actually Produce 12 Volts?

How Does a 12-Volt Solar Panel Work?

A 12V solar panel works by converting sunlight directly into DC electricity through photovoltaic cells. The electrical output can then power a compatible DC load or pass through a charge controller to recharge a battery.

Here is how the process works:

  1. Sunlight reaches the solar cells: Photons transfer energy to electrons inside the photovoltaic material.
  2. The cells generate DC electricity: The movement of these electrons creates electrical current.
  3. Connected cells provide usable voltage and current: The cell arrangement determines the panel’s electrical characteristics.
  4. A charge controller regulates battery charging: When a battery is in use, a compatible controller manages the current delivered to it.
  5. The battery stores energy: Stored solar electricity can supply power to loads when sunlight is low or absent.
  6. An inverter can supply AC loads: If the connected device requires AC (alternating current) rather than DC, a suitable inverter converts battery DC into AC.

Solar output changes with irradiance, shade, temperature, dust, and panel orientation. The wattage printed on a panel represents rated output under specified test conditions, not guaranteed output throughout the day.

How Much Power Does a 12V Solar Panel Produce?

A 12V solar panel can produce very different amounts of power depending on its wattage and current rating. The 12V label does not determine its wattage.

Electrical power can be calculated using a simple formula listed below:

Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A)

For example, if a 12V panel has a maximum-power voltage (Vmp) of 20V and a maximum-power current (Imp) of 5A, then the power it delivers is 100W (20V Vmp × 5A Imp).

How Many Amps Does a 12V Solar Panel Produce?

The current produced by a 12V solar panel depends on its power rating (wattage) and maximum power voltage (Vmp). A higher-wattage panel will produce more current than a lower-wattage panel operating at a similar voltage.

You can estimate the current using the formula listed below:

Current (A) = Power (W) ÷ Voltage (V)

For example, if a 100W 12V solar panel has a Vmp of 20V, then 100W ÷ 20V = 5A.

So, the panel produces about 5A at its maximum power point.

The exact current should always be checked in the panel’s datasheet under Imp, or current at maximum power. Do not calculate it by simply dividing the wattage by the nominal 12V rating because the panel’s actual operating voltage is usually higher.

The panel current and the battery-charging current can also differ. An MPPT (maximum power point tracking) charge controller can convert the panel’s higher voltage into a lower charging voltage while increasing the available charging current.

What Is a Thin Film Solar Cell?

A thin film solar panel is built from thin film solar cells, manufactured by stacking several layers of light-absorbing material on top of one another. Because these layers absorb light so effectively, they don’t need to be thick, which keeps the whole panel lightweight, flexible, and easy to work with.

In practice, some solar panel manufacturers even produce these layers as a solar film sheet. It’s a thin, flexible sheet of photovoltaic material that can be applied to curved or unconventional surfaces where a rigid glass panel simply wouldn’t fit.

How Does a Thin Film Photovoltaic Solar Panel Work?

Each thin film photovoltaic cell contains P-type and N-type semiconductor material. The P-type layer has fewer free electrons, while the N-type layer has plenty of them. When sunlight strikes the panel, electrons get energised and move across the P-N junction, generating an electric current.

This current either powers appliances directly or gets stored in a battery for later use. While traditional crystalline panels rely almost entirely on silicon, a thin film solar module can use materials like copper, indium, gallium, selenide, amorphous silicon, or cadmium telluride instead.

Types of Thin Film Solar Cells

There are three well-established types of thin film solar cells in use today, along with newer technology still making its way to market.

1. Amorphous Solar Cells (a-Si)

These cells are made of silicon and are widely used for their abundance and low cost. They are very thin, and one layer is the size of one micrometre.

2. Copper Gallium Indium Diselenide (CGID)

These cells are made of Copper, Indium, and Selenide, layered on top of each other. The thin layer of these thin-film solar panel cells provides flexibility.

3. Cadmium Telluride (CdTe)

These are the most popular thin-film solar panel cells used today. They are made from thin layers of cadmium telluride. They can absorb sunlight at shorter wavelengths than traditional solar cells. Wavelengths that are more concise help in easily converting sunlight into electricity.

4. Emerging Technology: Perovskite Cells

Perovskite thin film cells are still largely in the research and early-commercial stage. They’ve shown impressive efficiency gains in lab conditions, but haven’t yet reached the same level of large-scale, proven commercial deployment as CdTe or CIGS.

How Are Thin Film Solar Panels Manufactured?

Manufacturing starts with aluminium foil coils rolled through large presses, similar to how newspapers are printed. The machine presses these rolls into long sheets, and a printer applies a thin layer of semiconducting ink onto the coating, in an open-air setting.

This open-air printing approach is a real step up from older CIGS-on-glass or CdTe cell fabrication methods. It is faster, simpler, and considerably cheaper. Another layer of CdS and ZnO is added to ensure that sunlight can permeate through the semiconductor layer.

After this, the foil is laid down and cut into sheets. In this process, very little waste is generated, making the process highly efficient and cutting down electricity costs.

Thin Film Solar Panels Efficiency

Thin film solar panels efficiency is between ~6% and ~18%, depending on the technology used. CdTe (Cadmium Telluride) panels tend to sit at the higher end of that range, amorphous silicon at the lower end, with CIGS (Copper Indium Gallium Di selenide) falling somewhere in between.

This lower efficiency isn’t necessarily a dealbreaker. Since thin film panels are cheaper to produce, the trade-off often works out fine for large installations with plenty of open space, even if it means covering more area for the same output as a smaller crystalline system. For a sprawling solar farm or a large commercial rooftop, that trade-off between efficiency and cost per watt can genuinely work out in thin film’s favour.

Do Thin Film Panels Perform Better in Hot Weather?

Thin film panels generally hold up a bit better in high heat than crystalline silicon, since their output tends to drop less steeply as temperatures rise. That said, this varies by technology, and it isn’t accurate to assume every thin film panel automatically outperforms every crystalline panel once the temperature climbs.

Advantages of Thin Film Solar Cells

  • They are very flexible, making them easy to use in various applications.
  • They are lighter, and mass production requires less capital.
  • They are environmentally friendly because they contain less percentage of silicon. Thus, there are fewer emissions during their production.
  • They work even on cloudy days. The thin-film solar cells have a variety of layers for absorbing light of varying degrees. Thus, they can produce electricity in cloudy, rainy weather by absorbing ultraviolet radiation.

Limitations of Thin Film Solar Panels

Some of the limitations of thin-film photovoltaic solar panels are:

  • The thin-film solar cells are less efficient, converting only 20 to 30% of the solar energy into electricity.
  • More space is required for their installation.
  • They also absorb more heat, thereby making heat retention higher. Thin-film solar panels are placed directly under the sunlight. Hence, they take a long time to cool down.

Thin film’s light weight and flexibility open up uses that rigid crystalline panels can’t easily match:

For most Indian homes, a thin film solar panel isn’t usually the first choice, and that comes down to simple math. What Is a DCR Solar Panel? 

DCR stands for Domestic Content Requirement. A solar panel qualifies as DCR-compliant when both the solar cells and the complete module are manufactured in India.

These rules apply to certain government schemes, subsidies, and tender. Their purpose is to direct public spending towards solar equipment manufactured within the country.

What Makes a Solar Panel DCR-Compliant? 

In order to be DCR-compliant, a panel must meet both of the following conditions:

  • The solar cells must be made in India.
  • The solar module must be made in India.

In simple terms, a module assembled in India using imported solar cells does not qualify as DCR-compliant.

The origin of the solar cells is especially important. A panel may carry an Indian brand name and may even be assembled at an Indian factory. However, it will still fail to meet DCR requirements when the cells used inside it are imported.

Solar panel manufacturers and installers must provide consumers with documents that confirm the origin of both the cells and the module. Merely assembling imported cells into a panel in India does not make it DCR-compliant.

Why Did India Introduce DCR Rules? 

India introduced DCR requirements to support domestic solar manufacturing and reduce dependence on imported equipment.

The main objectives include:

  • Increase local manufacturing: The government aims to encourage companies to manufacture solar cells and modules within India.
  • Reduce import dependence: It will lower the country’s reliance on foreign solar equipment and components.
  • Create jobs: It will support employment across solar cell production, module manufacturing, and related industries.
  • Strengthen the supply chain: It will develop a domestic supply network that remains stable during import restrictions, price increases, or global disruptions.
  • Direct government support: The government wants to ensure that subsidies and public tenders benefit Indian-made equipment.

Is DCR a Solar Panel Technology?

DCR is a policy and manufacturing-origin classification, not a panel technology. It shows whether the solar cells and modules meet the required domestic manufacturing conditions. It does not indicate the panel’s efficiency, output, lifespan, or cell technology.

A DCR-compliant solar panel may use:

  • TOPCon (Tunnel Oxide Passivated Contact) technology
  • Monofacial construction
  • Bifacial construction
  • Different wattages and efficiency ratings

Two DCR panels can therefore have completely different performance levels. Their electricity generation, degradation rate, temperature performance, and lifespan depend on their technology, materials, and technical specifications.

A Non-DCR solar panel does not meet the domestic manufacturing conditions required under DCR rules. In most cases, this is because the panel uses solar cells manufactured outside India.

Non-DCR panels can be purchased and installed in projects where DCR compliance is not mandatory. However, they will not qualify for subsidies, government schemes, or tenders that specifically require domestically manufactured cells and modules.

  • Use imported solar cells

  • Be assembled in India using imported cells
  • Use a combination of Indian and imported components that does not meet DCR conditions

The Non-DCR label does not indicate poor quality or lower performance. Neither is a non-DCR solar panel illegal to use in India.

How Does TOPCon Technology Work?

A silicon solar cell converts sunlight into electricity by releasing electrons inside the silicon. These electrons must reach the metal contacts before they can be collected as usable current.

In P-type solar cells, some electrons are lost before reaching the contacts. They recombine with holes inside the cell, reducing the amount of electricity the panel can generate.

TOPCon technology reduces this loss in two ways:

  • By adding an ultra-thin tunnel oxide layer
  • By adding a layer of doped silicon at the rear of the cell

Together, these layers create a more efficient path for electrons to reach the contact while limiting recombination.

Because fewer electrons are lost, more electricity leaves the cell. This is one of the main reasons TOPCon cells can achieve efficiencies of around 25-26%, compared with 22.5% efficiency output from PERC cells.

TOPCon Solar Cell Structure

From the front, or sunlight-facing side, to the rear, a TOPCon solar cell contains the following layers:

The ultra-thin tunnel oxide and doped polysilicon layers are the key features of TOPCon technology. Together, they form a passivated contact that allows electrons to move towards the metal contact while reducing recombination losses.

The name comes from the ultra-thin oxide layer at the rear of the TOPCon solar cell.

What Is Solar Photovoltaic System?

A solar photovoltaic or solar PV system is a technology that converts sunlight directly into electricity through photovoltaic effect. ‘Photo’ means light, and ‘voltaic’ means electricity which means, a technology that produces electricity from light, specifically from the sun.

How Does a Solar PV System Work?

Before knowing the types, components, of the solar PV system, we need to understand the basic working principle to know how a solar PV system works.

Every solar panel is made up of dozens of solar cells that are made up of silicon. Each solar cell has two layers of silicon, positively charged and negatively charged. When sunlight hits the solar cells, photons (particles of light) knock electrons loose from the silicon atom. The two types of silicon layer built-in electric field at the junction due to which electrons move in a specific direction, creating a flow of DC electricity.

This is the photovoltaic effect in action. It happens silently, continuously, and with no moving parts which is a big part of why solar systems are so low-maintenance.

  • Energy independence: With a hybrid system and battery storage, your home can run on solar power even during grid outages insulating you from power cuts and rising tariff rates.
  • Scalability Solar PV systems can be installed at any scale from a 1kW system for a small apartment to a 500kW system for a large factory. You can also add panels as your energy needs grow.

Disadvantages of Solar Photovoltaic Systems

A Solar PV panel system also has some drawbacks, such as:

  • Intermittent generation: Solar panels generate more electricity when the sun is shining bright. Output drops a little on cloudy days and goes further lower at night. On-grid systems handle this seamlessly through the grid, but off-grid systems need adequate battery capacity to handle gaps in generation. Hybrid system works best if you want a combination of both the higher generation of electricity at day time and power back up when at night there is power outage.
  • Roof space requirement: A typical 1kW solar system requires about 10 square metres of unshaded roof space. For larger systems, this can become a constraint especially in apartments or buildings with shared or limited rooftop access.
  • Performance degrades over time: Solar panels lose efficiency gradually typically 0.5-0.7% per year. After 25 years, most panels are operating at around 80% of their original rated output. This is well within acceptable ranges for most households, but it’s worth accounting for when you do long-term planning.
  • Energy storage adds cost: If you want 24*7 solar-powered electricity (hybrid system), you need batteries, which are expensive. A quality lithium-ion battery bank adds Rs. 1-3 lakh or more to the system cost, depending on capacity and a few other factors of the system.
  • Grid dependency for on-grid systems during outages: The most common type of solar system in India (on-grid) doesn’t protect you from power cuts. For backup during outages, a hybrid system with battery storage is required.

Conclusion: Is a Solar PV System Worth It in India?

Yes, the Solar PV system is worth it for most homeowners in India.

India’s solar power potential is assessed at 10,830 GW, and with about 300 clear and sunny days a year, the calculated solar energy incidence on India’s land area is about 5,000 trillion kilowatt-hours per year. This is one of the most solar-rich countries in the world. That natural advantage, combined with affordable panel prices, rising grid tariffs, and government subsidies, makes solar PV one of the smartest long-term investments an Indian homeowner can make today.

The payback period for most residential systems is 3-5 years. After that, you’re generating almost free electricity for another 20 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it possible to use solar energy throughout the day?

Yes, it is possible to use solar energy for 24 hours. Crescent Dunes in Nevada is the first solar power plant that operates throughout the day.

Does solar power function during the night?

Yes, PV solar power functions during the night as well. The battery bank stores the excess energy in the power grid, and solar power utilises it in the dark.

Is UV light required for solar panel photovoltaic operation?

Solar PV panels mainly transform visible light into electricity but may also utilise about half of thermal light. However, PV solar panels require less amount of UV light for the solar power process.

What is a solar photovoltaic system?

A solar photovoltaic (PV) system converts sunlight into electricity using the photovoltaic effect. It consists of solar panels, an inverter, mounting structures, wiring, and optional batteries and a net meter.

What are the main components of a solar PV system?

The key components are solar panels, an inverter, a mounting structure, wiring and junction boxes, a monitoring system, and (in hybrid and off-grid systems) a battery bank and charge controller.

How long do solar PV systems last?

Solar panels typically last 25-30 years, with most manufacturers offering a 25-year performance warranty guaranteeing at least 80% of original output. Inverters generally last 10-15 years and sometimes may need replacement during the panel’s lifetime.

Can solar PV systems work during power cuts?

Standard on-grid solar systems shut off during power cuts for safety reasons. Hybrid solar systems with battery storage continue to power your home even when the grid goes down.
