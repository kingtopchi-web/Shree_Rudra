When sunlight hits a solar panel, the photovoltaic effect causes electrons to move, creating an electrical pressure that is generally referred to as the solar panel voltage and is measured in volts. In rooftop solar systems, you often see solar panels classified as 12V, 24V, or even 48V. However, those are nominal voltages, and not exactly the output a solar panel produces at a given moment in real time under real conditions. 

In reality, the solar panel voltage is of four main types: 

  • Nominal voltage
  • Actual operating voltage 
  • Maximum power voltage (Vmp)
  • Open-circuit voltage (Voc)

While nominal voltage is the standardized voltage that’s used to classify solar panels (usually, 12V, 24V, or 48V), the actual operating voltage of a solar panel is different. It’s the voltage generated by a solar panel in real-time, and is highly influenced by environmental factors such as solar radiation and temperature. 

One of the most significant factors influencing the actual operating voltage is the number of solar cells present in a solar panel. 

Usually, a single solar cell voltage output can range from 0.5V to 0.6V. Based on whether the solar panel has 60 or 72 cells, the total solar panel output voltage differs. For instance, if we keep aside other environmental factors, the actual solar panel output of a module with 72 solar cells can range between 36 volts and 43 volts. 

In this blog, we will walk you through the ins and outs of solar panel voltage, including types of solar panel voltages, tips to calculate the volts generated by different wattage solar panels, factors that influence a panel’s voltage, and the advantages of high-voltage solar panels. 

We will also spill the beans on the cost of installing an on-grid rooftop solar panel system for homes and housing societies that use high-efficiency solar panels. 

TL;DR Summary Box: How Many Volts is a Solar Panel?

A solar panel produces a voltage equal to the number of silicon solar cells present in the solar panel multiplied by the individual cell voltage. The usual 12-volt, 24-volt, and 48-volt solar panel outputs you usually see are the nominal voltages, which indicate the system voltage category for which a panel is designed. It is different from the actual volts a solar panel can produce in real-time. 

The actual solar panel output voltage depends on the number of cells connected in series within the panel structure. 

For simplicity, we’ve created this quick snapshot of how many volts a solar panel can produce: 

  • 60-cell panels: 30-36 volts (60 × 0.5-0.6V)
  • 72-cell panels: 36-43 volts (72 × 0.5-0.6V)
  • Half-cut cell panels: They maintain similar voltage with improved current characteristics.
  • Bifacial solar panels: They have the same voltage as standard panels with additional rear-side generation.

Please note: We have not taken into account the multiple factors that influence the actual solar panel voltage while making these calculations. The actual solar panel voltage output can vary significantly based on factors such as the strength of sunlight, solar panel efficiency, and the cell technology used.

What Is the Meaning of Solar Panel Voltage? 

Solar panel voltage is the electrical potential produced when sunlight creates an electric field inside the solar cells. It is measured in volts (V) and represents the pressure that pushes current through a circuit.

The solar panel output voltage depends on multiple important factors:

  • Cell configuration: Connecting more cells in series increases the open-circuit voltage (Voc) and the voltage at maximum power (Vmp). Parallel connections mainly increase current, not voltage.
  • Cell technology: Mono-PERC and TOPCon cells deliver higher voltage per cell than older polycrystalline cells. Thin-film modules have a lower voltage per cell, but they use multiple cells to achieve the required module voltage.
  • Temperature changes: As cell temperature rises, voltage falls. Cooler conditions raise voltage. This effect is described by the negative temperature coefficient, as specified in the datasheet provided by solar panel manufacturers.
  • Irradiance and shading: Strong sunlight helps the module operate near its maximum power point. Partial shading can activate bypass diodes, thereby reducing the effective voltage.
  • Load conditions: With no load, the panel sits at Voc. When connected to an inverter or solar charge controller, it operates near Vmp, which is lower than Voc.

Solar panels generate direct current (DC) voltage, which differs from the alternating current (AC) voltage used in homes. A solar inverter converts the DC voltage to AC for household use or grid connection.

What Are the Different Types of Solar Panel Voltages?

Solar panels have four primary voltage specifications: Open-circuit voltage (Voc), maximum power voltage (Vmp), actual operating voltage, and nominal voltage. Each solar panel voltage type refers to a different condition and helps match panels with inverters, charge controllers, and battery systems.

Let’s understand what each type means and does:

  • Open-circuit voltage (Voc): This is the highest voltage a panel can produce with no load connected and no current flowing. Measured under Standard Test Conditions, Voc occurs when the positive and negative terminals are completely disconnected. This voltage is critical for determining the maximum voltage rating requirements for inverters, charge controllers, and other electrical components. 
  • Maximum power voltage (Vmp): This is the voltage at which the solar panel produces its maximum power output, representing the optimal operating point. Vmp usually ranges from 80-85% of the Voc and is the voltage at which modern MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) charge controllers and inverters aim to operate the panel. This is the most important voltage for calculating actual system performance and energy production. 
  • Actual operating voltage: This is the real-time voltage that a solar panel produces under current environmental conditions and load requirements. Unlike Voc and Vmp, which are measured under Standard Test Conditions, the actual operating voltage fluctuates throughout the day based on factors such as temperature, sunlight intensity, shading, and the connected load or inverter’s MPPT tracking. On a hot afternoon, the actual operating voltage might be 5-10% lower than the rated Vmp due to temperature effects. In the early morning with cool temperatures and bright sunlight, it could be higher than the rated Vmp.
  • Nominal voltage: This classification indicates the system voltage category (12V, 24V, or 48V) for which a panel is designed, primarily for compatibility with lithium batteries and DC loads. Nominal voltage doesn’t represent the actual operating voltage of the panel. A 12V nominal panel actually operates at 17-18V for Vmp and 21-22V for Voc. The nominal rating is a compatibility label rather than an electrical measurement, enabling users to match panels with the appropriate system components.

How Much Voltage Does a Single Solar Cell Produce?

A single silicon solar cell can generate 0.5 to 0.6 volts under Standard Test Conditions, regardless of the cell’s physical size or power rating. This voltage is determined by the bandgap energy of the silicon material and the physics of the photovoltaic effect.

The single solar cell voltage output remains constant across different cell sizes because of the following reasons:

  • Material properties: Silicon’s bandgap sets the voltage range the cell can reach under sunlight.
  • Junction physics: The p-n junction fixes the cell’s operating voltage within a narrow band.
  • Cell design: Doping material and layer structures are similar across makers. Hence, solar cell voltages are close.
  • Manufacturing consistency: Industrial processes maintain key parameters, such as emitter and base doping, junction depth, and surface passivation quality, which limit variation in solar cell voltage output.

How Many Volts Does a Solar Panel Produce?

The actual volts a solar panel can produce depend on the number of solar cells present in the panel. 

Here’s the formula that can be used to calculate the solar panel output voltage: 

Solar panel output voltage = Number of cells x individual cell voltage.

Solar Panel Voltage for Different-Wattage Solar Panels

When solar panels use the same number of cells in series, their voltage remains in a similar range, regardless of their wattage size. Higher wattage mostly comes from higher current due to a larger area or higher-efficiency solar cells. 

Although higher wattage solar panels produce more power, their voltage doesn’t increase proportionally. Instead, they achieve higher power through increased current from more efficient solar cells.

To calculate the solar panel voltage of solar panels of any wattage, we use the same formula:

Solar panel output voltage = Number of cells x individual cell voltage.

How many volts a solar panel produces will depend on the number of solar cells present in the module: 

  • How many volts is a 300-watt solar panel? If the panel has 60 solar cells in series and each cell has a voltage of 0.5V, the theoretical Voc is 30V. 
  • How many volts is a 400-watt solar panel? If the panel has 72 solar cells in series and each cell has a voltage of 0.5V, the theoretical Voc is 36V. 
  • How many volts does a 500-watt solar panel produce? If the panel has 72 solar cells in series and each cell has a voltage of 0.6V, the theoretical Voc is 43V.

What Are the Factors that Affect the Solar Panel Voltage?

Solar panel voltage changes throughout the day. Cell temperature has the biggest effect because voltage drops as cells heat and rises in cold weather. Multiple other factors that influence the solar panel voltage include the strength of sunlight, the number of solar cells in the panel, shading, the age of the panel, and the cell technology used. 

Let’s understand how each factor affects the solar panel output voltage:

  • Temperature affects voltage: Voltage decreases as cells heat up and increases in cold weather. During string sizing, the cold-day open-circuit voltage (Voc) must be calculated to ensure that the string remains below the inverter or charge controller’s maximum input voltage.
  • Sunlight level (irradiance): More sunlight increases current significantly. Voltage, on the other hand, changes only a little. Hence, the operating voltage shifts slightly with the light level, while most of the power gain comes from the higher current.  
  • Partial shading: Shade can activate bypass diodes inside the panel. This cuts the affected section out of the circuit, lowering the string voltage.
  • Soiling, dust, or snow: Deposits on the panels block light, creating uneven illumination. This reduces operating voltage and can trigger bypass diodes.
  • Number of cells in series: More cells in series give a higher voltage. Panels with the same number of series cells have similar voltages.
  • Cell technology and temperature coefficient: Different cell types have different voltage temperature coefficients. This changes the amount of voltage that drops as the temperature of the cells rises.
  • Orientation, tilt, and mounting gap: These set how hot the cells run under real conditions. A hotter operation lowers the voltage, and better cooling raises it.
  • Wind and ambient conditions: Wind cools solar panels and can raise voltage. On the other hand, high ambient temperatures increase cell temperature and reduce voltage.
  • MPPT operating point: The inverter or charge controller holds the solar array near the maximum power point. The real-time operating voltage is wherever the MPPT tracks.
  • Wiring and connector losses: Resistance in DC cables and connections causes a voltage drop under load. 
  • Aging and degradation: Effects such as LID, LeTID, and PID reduce performance over time. Operating voltage can decline as the module ages. Every solar panel has a degradation rate.

Why Does Solar Panel Voltage Matter So Much in Designing Solar Systems?

Solar panel voltage is crucial because it determines how panels connect to inverters or charge controllers, whether the array remains within safe limits in cold weather, and how efficiently power flows through the wiring. Needless to say, getting solar panel voltage output right protects the solar system equipment components and improves energy yield across the day, making sure the rooftop solar system generates maximum possible solar electricity.

Let’s check out all the reasons that make solar panel voltage so critical: 

  • It keeps strings within safe limits: Cold weather raises open-circuit voltage (Voc). String length must be chosen so the cold-day Voc stays below the inverter’s maximum DC input. 
  • It keeps the array inside the MPPT window: The array voltage needs to stay within the inverter’s MPPT window and above its start-up level. If the voltage is lower, the inverter stays off, and no energy is captured.
  • It reduces losses in the DC wiring: A higher string voltage moves the same power with less current. Less current means less voltage drop and less heat in the wires. 
  • It determines how many panels can fit in a string: The series voltage is the sum of the module Voc values. Hence, the module count must be set to meet both the cold-day maximum and the MPPT operating range.
  • It provides headroom under partial shade: Partial shade can activate bypass diodes and reduce the voltage of the shaded section. The string should, therefore, be designed with sufficient voltage so that the inverter remains within its MPPT range.
  • It matches the lithium battery’s charging in off-grid solar systems and hybrid solar systems: In off-grid and hybrid systems, the PV string voltage must remain within the MPPT controller’s input window. That’s because charging current flows only when the solar panel’s operating voltage is higher than the battery’s charge voltage.

What Are the Advantages of High-Voltage Solar Panels?

High-voltage solar panels reduce current flow, minimize wiring expenses, and decrease electrical losses. As a result, they’re highly preferred in both residential and commercial rooftop solar systems. 

Let’s check out all the advantages of high-voltage solar panels that have made them the leading contenders for on-grid rooftop solar systems for housing societies, homes, commercial complexes, and industries:

  • Reduced wiring costs: Higher voltage carries the same power with less current. So, thinner copper cables can be used. This has a significant impact on the overall cost of the final solar system installation.
  • Lower labor cost: Fewer parallel connections and combiner boxes mean less wiring work. This will reduce the installation time, which, in turn, reduces labor costs.
  • Improved electrical efficiency: Lower current means less heat loss in DC wires. This can increase the overall efficiency by 1-3%. This 1-3% might look small on the surface, but it will add thousands of kilowatt-hours over 25 years and improve returns.
  • Simpler design: With fewer parallel strings, fewer combiners, and fewer joints, there are fewer parts that can fail. Troubleshooting and maintenance are easier throughout the system’s life.

FAQs

Q1. Are solar panels 12V or 24V?

Ans. 12 V and 24 V are nominal voltage labels used mainly for battery systems. A 12 V panel operates at around 17-18 V at maximum power, and many modern solar panels for grid-tied systems have Vmp in the 30-40 V range.

Q2. What are solar amps and watts?

Ans. Amps (A) is the electric current a solar panel delivers. Watts (W), on the other hand, is the power produced and is calculated as Volts × Amps. Higher current or higher voltage increases watts.

Q3. Is solar panel output AC or DC?

Ans. The output from a solar panel is always DC (direct current). A solar inverter converts the DC to AC for household or grid usage.

Q4. Do all solar panels put out 12 volts?

Ans. No. Only 12 V nominal panels are designed for that category, and they actually operate around 17-18 V at maximum power.
